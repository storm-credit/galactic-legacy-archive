## 1. Core principle

There is no single “underworld.”

Crime arises where:
- valuable goods move across jurisdictions;
- identity and title can diverge;
- routes create chokepoints;
- legal service is expensive or unavailable;
- war and sanctions create shortages;
- states/corporations outsource coercion;
- people need medicine, food, parts or movement faster than law allows.

A smuggler, pirate, corrupt broker, salvage fraudster and mercenary may trade with one another without belonging to one organization.

---

## 2. Legal-market spectrum

### L0 — fully licensed trade

- declared cargo;
- current title/certification;
- taxes/fees paid;
- recognized insurer/route access.

### L1 — regulatory gray market

- legal goods moved through unofficial channels;
- expired paperwork, tax avoidance, unauthorized repair, informal labor.

### L2 — necessity smuggling

- restricted medicine, identity documents, refugees, sanctioned parts or food moved outside formal law.

Moral status:
- depends on what is moved, for whom, and at whose risk.

### L3 — illicit commercial market

- stolen goods;
- forged certification;
- weapons rights;
- exploitative debt;
- data theft;
- counterfeit medicine/parts.

### L4 — coercive criminal enterprise

- protection rackets;
- kidnapping;
- extortion;
- trafficking;
- violent seizure;
- forced debt labor.

### L5 — organized piracy / armed predation

- attacks transport and settlements for cargo, ransom, ship seizure or political leverage.

Rule:
- one actor can move between levels by jurisdiction and event; classification is not personality alignment.

## 3. Major illicit markets

### 3.1 Ship and frame title laundering

Crime:
- stolen/captured hull converted into apparently lawful property through forged salvage, insurance or war-loss records.

Needed infrastructure:
- corrupt title broker;
- physical serial modification;
- false maintenance lineage;
- insurer/port acceptance;
- crew willing to operate it.

Archive relevance:
- provenance errors can outlive the criminals and become historical truth.

### 3.2 Counterfeit parts and certifications

Drivers:
- embargo;
- high certified prices;
- obsolete equipment;
- urgent repair.

Danger:
- fake part may physically fit while failing heat/control/authentication layers.

This connects directly to existing C5 false-compatibility logic.

### 3.3 Medical / augmentation black market

Goods:
- neural stabilizers;
- prosthetic parts;
- gene treatments;
- unlicensed implant service;
- identity-linked drugs;
- fertility/gestation access.

Conflict:
- some markets save excluded people;
- others exploit dependency with dangerous substitutes and data theft.

### 3.4 Identity and citizenship fraud

Products:
- temporary worker identities;
- forged death/closure records;
- family/guardian records;
- route credentials;
- military discharge/service papers.

Rule:
- identity fraud must not become magic disguise. Biometrics, witnesses, history, service logs and current relationships all create friction.

### 3.5 Route-slot / cargo fraud

- false manifests;
- priority manipulation;
- phantom cargo;
- stolen queue credentials;
- misdeclared hazard class;
- bribed inspectors.

### 3.6 Salvage crime

- claiming live survivors/assets as wreckage;
- falsifying abandonment;
- stripping evidence;
- moving weapons before legal custody;
- manipulating battlefield exclusion zones.

GA2 fit:
- especially strong.

### 3.7 Data / Archive / evidence market

- forged provenance;
- stolen records;
- witness intimidation;
- selective deletion;
- leaked medical/command data.

No single hacker can rewrite all institutions.

## 4. Smuggling logistics

Smuggling is a logistics problem.

Methods:
- false cargo category;
- hidden compartments;
- legitimate cargo mixing;
- transshipment through weak jurisdiction;
- identity substitution;
- emergency/rescue exemptions;
- corrupt berth assignment;
- unregistered small craft;
- physical handoff outside major nodes.

Limits:
- mass/heat/power signatures;
- route queues;
- crew knowledge;
- insurance;
- inspection randomness;
- buyer trust;
- spoilage/medicine conditions.

Rule:
- “secret route” does not automatically bypass lattice physics.

## 5. Piracy ecology

Piracy requires:
- targets;
- intelligence;
- refuge/repair;
- buyers for cargo;
- ransom/payment channels;
- corrupt or weak jurisdictions;
- crews willing to accept retaliation risk.

### Pirate models

#### P-A Opportunist raiders

- small craft;
- hit weak routes;
- low political structure.

#### P-B Protection racket fleet

- extracts regular payment from settlements/convoys;
- may provide real security against others.

#### P-C War-surplus privateer drift

- former military/authorized privateers whose legal war ended or patron collapsed.

#### P-D Political-pirate hybrid

- armed seizures fund rebellion/claimant/local cause.

#### P-E Corporate/state deniable predation

- looks like piracy but client benefits strategically.

Control:
- pirates do not form one universal “Pirate Nation.”

---

## 6. Why piracy is not everywhere

Counterweights:
- convoy economics;
- patrols;
- insurance;
- port exclusion;
- transponder/evidence chains;
- retaliation;
- limited repair/ammunition;
- difficult resale of distinctive ships/frames;
- crew defection;
- neutral arbitration.

A pirate victory can still be financially bad if loot cannot be serviced or sold.

---

## 7. Organized crime structures

Possible durable structures:

- port broker networks;
- debt/enforcement syndicates;
- salvage houses;
- transport families/crews;
- counterfeit workshops;
- data brokers;
- armed protection groups;
- corrupt legal/insurance intermediaries.

Each needs:
- revenue source;
- enforcement;
- succession;
- legitimate front;
- political protection;
- vulnerability.

No named syndicate is canonized here.

# PART II — JUSTICE AND ENFORCEMENT

## 8. Enforcement layers

### J1 Local civil police / habitat security

Handles:
- violence;
- theft;
- local fraud;
- public safety;
- missing persons.

### J2 Port/customs authority

Handles:
- cargo;
- customs;
- quarantine;
- vessel documentation;
- hazardous goods;
- immigration/entry.

### J3 Specialized financial / insurance investigators

Handles:
- laundering;
- claim fraud;
- title fraud;
- corporate crime.

### J4 Military police / service investigators

Handles:
- military property;
- desertion;
- service crime;
- war-zone custody.

### J5 Imperial/state intelligence-security

Handles:
- espionage;
- strategic sabotage;
- succession/continuity threats.

Risk:
- expanding security crime into ordinary politics.

### J6 Neutral / cross-jurisdiction arbitration and custody

Handles:
- claims;
- ship disputes;
- protected evidence;
- cross-border custody where parties accept standing.

No one layer owns all crime.

---

## 9. Jurisdiction matrix

A case can involve:
- crime location;
- victim citizenship;
- suspect citizenship;
- ship registry;
- cargo title;
- port law;
- military status;
- contract forum;
- Neutral covenant;
- emergency authority.

Good crime stories use conflicting valid jurisdictions rather than one incompetent police force.

---

## 10. Investigation constraints

Evidence sources:
- physical traces;
- vessel logs;
- route timing;
- maintenance history;
- financial records;
- witnesses;
- medical data under warrant/consent rules;
- authentication records;
- cargo mass/energy inconsistencies.

Limits:
- communication delay;
- jurisdiction;
- privacy;
- corrupted/partial records;
- forged but plausible documents;
- inaccessible ports;
- witness fear.

Archive cannot serve as admissible omniscient evidence by itself.

---

## 11. Courts and dispute resolution

Possible forums:
- local criminal court;
- commercial court;
- military tribunal;
- Imperial appellate court;
- Neutral claims/arbitration chamber;
- emergency review panel;
- community/local-restorative process for limited offenses.

Rule:
- legal result and factual truth can diverge without making law meaningless.

---

## 12. Detention / punishment

Possible sanctions:
- restitution;
- fines;
- license suspension;
- asset seizure after adjudication;
- travel/route restrictions;
- supervised service;
- imprisonment;
- military discharge;
- corporate debarment;
- exile only under defined law;
- rehabilitation/treatment where appropriate.

Prohibitions:
- life-support prosthetics cannot be treated as ordinary seized property;
- species-incompatible detention cannot be used as punishment;
- family/identity closure cannot silently substitute for sentence.

The K-13 Academy’s correctional system remains a special institution, not the model for all justice.

---

## 13. Corruption model

Corruption may involve:
- bribes;
- patronage;
- selective enforcement;
- procurement kickbacks;
- family/house influence;
- insurer pressure;
- data manipulation;
- legal delay;
- unofficial immunity.

Important:
- corruption works through institutions with real services, not because everyone is evil.

# PART III — STORY / FACTION INTEGRATION

## 14. Mercenary boundary

Mercenary ≠ criminal.

A licensed company can be lawful.
A gray contractor can commit crimes.
A pirate can later receive conditional amnesty/privateer status.

The story should track:
- contract law;
- actual acts;
- jurisdiction;
- civilian harm;
not use occupation labels as morality.

---

## 15. Independence / smuggler boundary

Independence networks may rely on smugglers while also policing exploitative trafficking or theft.

Internal conflict:
- “necessary route” versus predatory criminal economy.

This gives FCT-04 more depth than heroic rebels with cool smugglers.

---

## 16. Helix / gray market boundary

Helix certification genuinely protects people from bad parts and medicine.

Its monopoly/price/access can also create black-market demand.

Therefore:
- gray market danger validates some Helix arguments;
- Helix exclusion validates some open/illegal alternatives.

Neither side owns the truth.

---

## 17. Species / multi-species crime

Do not racialize crime.

Species differences can affect:
- hiding spaces/body geometry;
- food/medicine smuggling needs;
- identity documents;
- life-support logistics;
- enforcement bias.

But no species is “naturally criminal.”

---

## 18. Frame/ship equipment consequences

Criminal/gray actors favor:
- common chassis;
- repairable older units;
- modular cargo space;
- scrubbed/forged IDs;
- civilian-looking craft;
- captured parts.

Unique superframes are poor criminal assets because:
- easy identification;
- expensive service;
- impossible resale.

This reinforces reuse-first equipment ecology.

## 20. Red-team stop conditions

Reject if:
- all smugglers are lovable rogues;
- all police are authoritarian;
- piracy has no repair/resale economy;
