## 0. Core rule

`Gray Layer` is not one underworld faction.

It is an overlapping economic/social layer containing actors whose legality changes by jurisdiction, client and cargo.

The same organization may be:
- licensed in one system;
- tolerated in another;
- sanctioned elsewhere;
- criminal under Imperial law;
- indispensable during disaster.

Avoid a single “crime syndicate controls everything” model.

---

## 1. Five durable archetypes

### G-01 Contract Combat Company

Business:
- escort, anti-raider work, limited assault, force protection, training.

Revenue:
- retainer contracts;
- mission fees;
- hazard bonuses;
- salvage clauses;
- training/advisory work between wars.

Cost stack:
- payroll;
- insurance;
- ammunition;
- propellant;
- maintenance;
- medevac;
- dependent benefits;
- licensing and bribery where necessary.

Equipment ecology:
- standardized surplus/common frames;
- a few specialist frames;
- mobile workshop;
- transport/tender capacity;
- limited heavy ships rented or client-supplied.

Failure mode:
- cash-flow death after one unpaid client.

### G-02 Security / Recovery Cooperative

Business:
- cargo recovery;
- hostage/patient extraction;
- abandoned-hull retrieval;
- convoy inspection;
- hazardous-site stabilization.

Legal texture:
- often licensed as salvage/security rather than military.

Equipment:
- rescue/service frames;
- boarding/security variants;
- tugs;
- medical and sensor craft.

Narrative value:
- can oppose protagonist legally while doing useful public work.

### G-03 Raider Brotherhood / Predatory Flotilla

Business:
- coercive tolls;
- cargo seizure;
- ransom;
- capture and resale;
- patron-sponsored disruption.

Material truth:
- raids are about cargo, fuel, hostages, parts and route leverage more often than annihilation.

Equipment:
- modified production frames;
- industrial capture tools;
- fast escorts;
- hidden tenders/repair contacts.

Weakness:
- medical scarcity, parts incompatibility, trust and succession.

Rule:
- raiders that destroy everything they could profit from need a political/ideological reason.

### G-04 Smuggler / Passage Network

Business:
- move sanctioned people, medicine, records, luxury goods, weapons, embryos/medical material or politically sensitive cargo.

Structure:
- cells, captains, brokers, safe ports and trusted service crews rather than one hierarchy.

Power:
- route knowledge and trust, not battlefield strength.

Equipment:
- civilian hulls;
- hidden cargo systems;
- modular life support;
- utility frames;
- authentication translation.

Species relevance:
- multi-species smuggling may require hidden atmosphere, diet, medical and waste-support modules, increasing cost and detection risk.

### G-05 Black-Market Technical Network

Business:
- uncertified parts;
- banned software;
- copied service manuals;
- forged but sometimes technically valid adapters;
- battlefield salvage;
- old-standard reconstruction.

Members:
- independent engineers;
- corrupt certified shops;
- former Helix/state workers;
- pirates;
- frontier cooperatives;
- collectors.

Moral ambiguity:
- can sell dangerous counterfeit components;
- can also keep isolated hospitals and ships alive when certified supply is blocked.

Equipment effect:
- major source of hybrid/field-modified machines but rarely origin of an entire clean production lineage.

## 2. Non-durable actors

Do not promote every temporary criminal crew into a faction.

Short-lived forms:
- one-job raider crew;
- bounty team;
- deserter band;
- sanction runner;
- local extortion cell;
- illegal race/combat group;
- stolen-hull crew.

These may become story opponents without receiving full institutional depth.

---

## 3. Contract grammar

Every story-visible mercenary contract should define:
- client;
- legal jurisdiction;
- objective;
- start/end condition;
- authorized force;
- target exclusions;
- salvage rights;
- casualty payment;
- medevac responsibility;
- ammunition responsibility;
- repair responsibility;
- intelligence ownership;
- surrender/withdrawal clause;
- payment security;
- dispute court/arbitrator.

A vague “they were hired to attack” contract is insufficient for a recurring organization.

---

## 4. Reputation system

Reputation is multi-dimensional:
- mission completion;
- civilian harm;
- surrender treatment;
- contract fidelity;
- information leakage;
- maintenance safety;
- casualty care;
- client default history.

A company can be:
- tactically excellent but legally radioactive;
- reliable but expensive;
- humane but unwilling to take high-risk assault work;
- cheap because it abandons wounded personnel.

This creates meaningful market differences.

---

## 5. Species and posthuman integration

Gray-layer organizations may be more mixed-species than formal military units because they recruit across borders, but this creates real costs:
- environment modules;
- medical spares;
- cockpit adapters;
- dietary logistics;
- translation;
- funeral and dependent-benefit law;
- incompatible cybernetic vendors.

Do not use “mercenary = tolerant” as automatic morality.

They may also exploit undocumented species/person status.

---

## 6. Frame ecology

Recommended gray-layer distribution:

### Common chassis 60–80%
- surplus Imperial/state models;
- local defense models;
- Helix licensed/expired variants;
- industrial conversions.

### Mission-modified chassis 15–30%
- boarding;
- EW;
- capture;
- long-endurance;
- rescue;
- low-signature.

### True unique hulls 0–5%
- only where structural donor combination, surviving prototype or extraordinary reconstruction passes the independent-chassis gate.

Rule:
- “custom paint + illegal gun” is not a new frame.

---

## 7. Relationship with 01–20 prototype history

Possible, but indirect:
- black-market manuals preserve a failed prototype subsystem;
- mercenary shops rediscover an old service adapter;
- a surviving prototype is temporarily owned by a company but becomes a financial/maintenance burden;
- production descendants become popular because they are repairable, not because mercenaries know secret ancient tech.

Hard veto:
- gray layer conveniently possesses all missing prototypes.

---

## 8. Political functions

Gray actors can:
- keep sanctioned communities supplied;
- worsen war through arms traffic;
- provide deniable state violence;
- undermine monopoly standards;
- exploit refugees;
- move witnesses and dissidents;
- create independent information markets;
- become proto-governments in abandoned regions.

One group may do several at once.

---

## 9. GA integration

GA1:
- minor brokers, salvage markets, fake relic rumors.

GA2:
- first serious visibility through contracts, ship acquisition, route risk and regional force ecology.

GA3:
- industrial sanctions create black-market substitution and unsafe competition.

GA4:
- claimants hire deniable forces; old houses use brokers; legitimacy conflict deepens.

GA5:
- mercenary and private ships face choice: paid contract versus temporary common-fleet trust.

GA6:
- evacuation prices, smuggling capacity and predation expose moral limits.

GA7:
- some networks become legitimate route institutions; others radicalize or collapse.

GA8–GA10:
- record/personhood systems redefine who is “criminal,” “licensed,” or “unrecognized.”

---

## 10. Stop conditions

Reject a gray-layer organization if:
- it has infinite ammunition/parts;
- it fights for fun without economic/political basis;
- it owns capital ships without support;
- all members share one moral alignment;
- every criminal is anti-state rebel;
- black-market tech is always better than certified tech;
- one boss controls the entire underworld;
- species identity substitutes for economic motive.

---

## 11. Recommended reader-facing envelope

Across the whole series:
- 2–3 recurring mercenary/security organizations;
- 2 major raider/pirate networks with different economic models;
- 2–4 smuggling/technical networks that may overlap geographically;
- many small one-arc crews without permanent registry status.

This is enough to make the galaxy feel economically alive without turning the story into a crime-faction catalog.
