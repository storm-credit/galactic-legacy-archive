## 1. Core Military Principle

War is fought for control of:

- routes and node windows;
- repair and production;
- trained people and command legitimacy;
- food, medicine, propellant and ammunition;
- authentication and current orders;
- habitats, civilians and political cooperation;
- evidence, records and the right to define an operation.

Destroying enemy hulls is one method, not the universal objective.

A fleet that wins a battle but loses its route, yard, support ships, crews or legitimacy may be strategically defeated.

## 2. Five Combat Scales

### Tactical contact

- 1–12 ships or several frame flights;
- minutes to hours;
- local objectives: escort, capture, rescue, sensor control, delay or escape.

### Task-group operation

- 8–30 combat ships plus support;
- days to weeks;
- one route, convoy, node or local system.

### Fleet operation

- 30–120 combat ships plus substantial support;
- weeks to months;
- several nodes or a regional corridor.

### Theater campaign

- 150–500 combat ships across several fleets and regions;
- months;
- route network, yards, populations and political coalitions.

### Civilizational synchronization/transition conflict

- many autonomous regional forces;
- no useful single front line;
- military actions compete with service, identity, record and authority operations.

## 3. Operational Domains

### Route and approach control

Ships:
- screen, blockade, convoy, intercept, carry and sustain forces.

Nodes:
- schedule, authenticate and constrain movement.

Intelligence:
- queue and logistics indicators reveal concentration before arrival.

### Infrastructure capture and service

Frames, utility craft and boarding teams:
- seize or repair nodes, docks, habitats, batteries and ships;
- operate where heavy weapons would destroy the objective.

### Civilian and service protection

- escort and evacuation;
- hospital, food, life-support and communication continuity;
- rules of engagement and public legitimacy.

### Command, authentication and information

- orders, identity, evidence, target classification and surrender terms;
- false or delayed orders can be as decisive as weapon fire.

### Industrial and personnel endurance

- yards, crews, maintainers, ammunition and rest;
- the campaign continues only if replacements and family obligations are managed.

## 4. Ship Role Families

### Picket / courier / scout

- sensors, messages, route observation and target confirmation;
- high strategic value, low direct survivability;
- destruction can blind or delay whole formations.

### Cutter / patrol craft

- customs, escort, boarding, rescue and local security;
- numerous and politically visible;
- weak in line combat.

### Corvette / light escort

- convoy screen, anti-missile, pursuit and local command;
- foundation of frontier and dispersed forces.

### Frigate

- independent patrol, escort leader, sensors/EW and limited node assault;
- balanced but not dominant.

### Destroyer

- missiles, fleet screening, anti-ship and local superiority;
- high ammunition and maintenance demand.

### Cruiser

- regional command, sustained operations, heavy sensors/weapons and independent political presence;
- can dominate small systems but cannot hold populations alone.

### Battleship / heavy carrier

- trunk-node battle, fleet command, siege deterrence and mass frame/craft support;
- rare, expensive, slow to replace and politically symbolic.

### Support families

- tankers and propellant ships;
- ammunition and countermeasure transports;
- repair tenders and mobile yards;
- hospital/rescue ships;
- cargo and troop/passenger transports;
- sensor/communication ships;
- tugs and salvage craft;
- food/water/life-support carriers;
- command and archive/evidence vessels.

Rule:
- support ships receive named roles and losses. They are not invisible logistics.

## 5. Frame Role Families

### Service / rescue frame

- hull work, node repair, casualty extraction and infrastructure access;
- often decisive when destruction is unacceptable.

### Utility / industrial frame

- cargo, construction, mining and emergency conversion;
- numerous but unevenly protected/trained.

### Patrol / security frame

- customs, boarding, habitat defense and local policing;
- limited range and heavy dependence on local structures.

### Fleet maneuver frame

- ship defense, close capture, sensor/tag deployment, anti-craft and infrastructure combat;
- cannot replace ships at strategic range.

### Assault / breaching frame

- hardened boarding, battery/node capture and close anti-ship work;
- high casualty, support and ammunition cost.

### Recon / electronic frame

- tags, relay, classification, decoys and local network warfare;
- weak if isolated.

### Command / coordination frame

- enhanced communication/sensors and limited local command;
- a target and network dependency, not a magical battlefield controller.

### Elite / custom frame

- optimized around one pilot, institution or mission;
- difficult to replace, repair and transfer;
- no universal superiority.

- service/capture/rescue frame with high pilot adaptability;
- mixed components enable unusual interoperability;
- limited long-range firepower and sustained combat;
- its strategic value grows through interface and mission authority, not only weapons.

## 6. Fleet Organization

### Frame element

- 2–4 frames.

### Frame flight

- 4–8 frames with one leader/support pairing.

### Frame squadron

- 12–24 frames plus carriers, maintenance and rescue.

### Ship section

- 2–4 similar ships for patrol/screen.

### Ship squadron

- 4–12 ships with one command role and attached support.

### Task group

Typical:
- 8–30 combat ships;
- 5–20 support ships;
- 30–150 frames/craft;
- 3,000–25,000 personnel depending composition.

### Fleet

Typical:
- 30–120 combat ships;
- 20–100 support ships;
- 150–800 frames/craft;
- 30,000–250,000 personnel.

### Grand/theater fleet

- several fleets under temporary theater authority;
- 150–500 combat hulls;
- equal or larger support train;
- months of assembly and visible logistics.

## 7. Command Roles

### Political mandate holder

- defines lawful objective, limits and resource commitment.

### Theater commander

- coordinates fleets and fronts within mandate.

### Fleet commander

- allocates task groups, logistics and engagement priorities.

### Ship master/captain

- ship navigation, safety, surrender and abandon-ship authority.

### Mission/flight commander

- tactical frame, boarding, rescue or defined operation.

### Logistics commander

- supply, repair, traffic and reserves.

### Medical/rescue authority

- treatment, casualty movement, quarantine and protected zones.

### Intelligence/data authority

- source confidence, targeting, authentication and evidence.

### Crew/local/civil representatives

- pay, family, surrender, withdrawal, local damage and political standing where institutionally recognized.

Rule:
- one person may hold several roles in small operations but the roles remain conceptually distinct and create accountability.

## 8. Command Styles

### Central detailed command

Strength:
- rapid coordination and massed effect.

Weakness:
- communication delay, false orders, local blindness and single-point capture.

### Command by intent

Strength:
- local adaptation and resilience.

Weakness:
- divergent interpretation and harder political control.

### Mission trust command

- participants lend bounded authority for objective/time;
- supports mixed ownership and political mandates;
- slower formation and fragile under ambiguity.

### Network/route coordination

- local commands share schedules, warnings, standards and minimum obligations;
- no universal commander;
- strong for dispersed protection, weak for sudden concentrated battle.

### Aurel/Continuity synchronization

- authenticates orders and aligns logistics across institutions;
- extremely effective when accepted;
- risks converting service coordination into political command.

## 9. Readiness Model

Every formation has separate readiness fields:

1. hull/structure;
2. propulsion and power;
3. sensors/communications;
4. weapons/ammunition;
5. frame/craft complement;
6. crew staffing and fatigue;
7. maintenance and spares;
8. food/medical/life support;
9. route and authentication;
10. legal/political mandate;
11. morale, family/pay and internal cohesion;
12. intelligence confidence.

Overall readiness is limited by the mission’s critical lowest fields, not an average percentage.

### Readiness bands

- R0 Inactive/laid up;
- R1 Transit/emergency only;
- R2 Limited mission;
- R3 Combat capable;
- R4 Sustained combat capable;
- R5 Theater ready with support.

A ship may be R4 mechanically and R1 legally or in crew cohesion.

## 10. Ammunition and Combat Endurance

### Point defense and countermeasures

- saturation can deplete stocks within one major engagement;
- heat and geometry matter.

### Missiles

- expensive, bulky and mission-specific;
- fleet combat may consume weeks/months of production in hours/days.

### Beam/energy weapons

- constrained by power, heat, optics and line of sight;
- ammunition-light but not cost-free.

### Kinetic weapons

- efficient but dangerous around infrastructure and persistent debris.

### Frame weapons/tools

- limited magazines, propellant, cooling and physical wear;
- recovery and rearming often determine repeat sortie rate.

Endurance rule:
- after 1–3 serious engagements without a repair/ammunition train, readiness falls sharply even if few hulls are destroyed.

## 11. Maintenance and Replacement

### Ship maintenance

- routine maintenance occurs continuously;
- battle damage requires yards/tenders, specialists and parts;
- old/mixed ships demand more labor and unique knowledge;
- cannibalization improves one unit while reducing the force pool.

### Frame maintenance

- sustained combat organizations require 4–12 direct maintainers per active frame plus shared specialists;
- pilot/interface health is part of readiness;
- custom frames have long replacement and retraining cycles.

### Crew replacement

- basic crew training: months;
- qualified specialists: years;
- captains, chief engineers, node operators and doctors cannot be replaced by generic manpower;
- casualties and fatigue change doctrine.

### Industrial replacement

Approximate peacetime-to-war lead:
- cutter: months;
- corvette/frigate: 1–3 years depending yard;
- destroyer: 2–5 years;
- cruiser: 4–9 years;
- capital ship: 8–20+ years;
- refit and conversion can be faster but creates compromises.

The 6-year main story cannot replace repeated mass fleet losses cleanly.

## 12. Imperial Doctrine

### Strategic aim

- preserve trunk routes, lawful command, populated systems and state-service continuity.

### Operational preferences

- layered route defense;
- authenticated centralized planning with delegated execution;
- combined fleet and node batteries;
- reserve concentration at L1/L2 nodes;
- protect public services and administrative legitimacy;
- use overwhelming force selectively rather than disperse everywhere.

### Strengths

- trained staffs;
- standard equipment;
- logistics and reserves;
- large-state intelligence and command;
- legal authority and local public services.

### Weaknesses

- slow political authorization;
- divided succession/office loyalty;
- dependence on authentication and standardized supply;
- provincial delay and local distrust;
- temptation to protect Core at frontier expense.

### Frame doctrine

- fleet escort, boarding, node/habitat capture and controlled infrastructure operations;
- elite units support decisive points but do not replace mass standard forces.

### Failure tendency

- valid but conflicting orders;
- local commanders wait or choose office allegiance;
- central preservation sacrifices remote routes.

## 13. Helix Security and Industrial Doctrine

### Strategic aim

- protect facilities, contracts, people, data, supply and certified systems.

### Operational preferences

- intelligence and legal pre-positioning;
- small high-readiness security units;
- route/berth/parts control;
- precision seizure and technical lockout;
- strong point defense and asset protection;
- hire state/local/merchant forces rather than build a universal navy.

### Strengths

- excellent maintenance and sensors;
- medical support;
- technical intelligence;
- rapid contract logistics;
- specialized frames and boarding teams.

### Weaknesses

- limited heavy combat mass;
- political legitimacy;
- internal division and investor/insurance constraints;
- dependence on state/Neutral route recognition;
- workers and local facilities can resist.

### Frame doctrine

- facility defense, recovery, boarding, technical seizure and high-survivability specialist operations.

### Failure tendency

- assumes ownership/technical control equals political control;
- sanctions create hostile alternatives and worker defection.

## 14. Independence and Localist Doctrine

### Strategic aim

- survive, deny monopoly, move people/evidence and secure local autonomy.

### Operational preferences

- dispersed cells and route knowledge;
- ambush, extraction, sabotage and political exposure;
- converted civilian/industrial assets;
- avoid symmetrical fleet battle;
- local concentration before central response;
- denial or destruction when capture appears inevitable.

### Strengths

- local support and intelligence;
- hidden routes;
- flexible command;
- irregular logistics;
- ability to disperse.

### Weaknesses

- incompatible equipment;
- limited medicine/ammunition/heavy repair;
- factional goals;
- weak sustained fleet coordination;
- civilians and communities bear reprisals/shortages.

### Frame doctrine

- industrial conversion, close infrastructure combat, extraction and local defense.

### Failure tendency

- destruction and militarization replace governance;
- liberation capacity is smaller than promises;
- cells act without affected-community consent.

## 15. Neutral Assembly Doctrine

### Strategic aim

- protect routes, rescue, custody, evidence and member trade without becoming a state navy.

### Operational preferences

- convoy and rescue corridors;
- protected medical zones;
- anti-seizure security;
- evidence and public legitimacy;
- captain/member autonomy;
- defensive concentration only under covenant.

### Strengths

- transport, rescue and claims legitimacy;
- diverse member assets;
- cross-border access;
- skilled captains and medical/logistics crews.

### Weaknesses

- no universal command;
- limited heavy combat;
- commercial and humanitarian splits;
- member withdrawal;
- inability to sustain occupation.

### Frame doctrine

- rescue, inspection, docking security and boarding defense.

### Failure tendency

- capacity rules exclude people;
- neutrality protects commerce more than justice;
- armed action risks the network’s value.

## 16. Worker, Ardis and Route-Federation Doctrine

### Strategic aim

- defend local services, yards, nodes, communities and negotiated autonomy.

### Operational preferences

- distributed fixed/local defense;
- utility frames and tugs;
- open-compatible logistics;
- mission trusts and local withdrawal rights;
- repair and denial rather than decisive annihilation;
- protect skilled workers and infrastructure.

### Strengths

- local physical knowledge;
- repair and production;
- plural command resilience;
- community legitimacy;
- mixed civilian/military assets.

### Weaknesses

- slow coalition and decision;
- ammunition and high-grade parts;
- vulnerable food/medical imports;
- local factionalism;
- difficult theater concentration.

### Frame doctrine

- service, node work, fixed defense and converted industrial mass supported by limited military specialists.

### Failure tendency

- technical/labor elites dominate;
- local withdrawal fragments collective defense;
- slower decisions cost lives.

## 17. Ownerless/Common Fleet Doctrine

### Strategic aim

- combine ships with different owners, crews, obligations and politics for bounded missions.

### Operational preferences

- mission-specific task groups;
- borrowed command authority;
- strong logistics/pay/repair tracking;
- decentralized execution under shared intent;
- explicit surrender, withdrawal and civilian limits;
- preserve formations rather than demand permanent loyalty.

### Strengths

- coalition scale;
- political flexibility;
- local expertise;
- resilience when central command fails;
- support and civilian institutions included in mandate.

### Weaknesses

- slow assembly;
- command disputes;
- uneven training and equipment;
- local withdrawal;
- fragile pay and shared ammunition.

### Failure tendency

- temporary command becomes personal authority;
- strongest fleet imposes terms;
- unpaid crews/local needs break mission trust.

## 18. Aurel/Preservation Doctrine

### Strategic aim

- preserve synchronized corridors, services, populations and command reliability.

### Operational preferences

- authenticated rapid concentration;
- logistics and service optimization;
- corridor defense and isolation of cascade risks;
- classification-based access and force;
- replace unreliable commands/offices;
- minimize visible destruction while controlling routes/services.

### Strengths

- high coordination and readiness;
- reliable pay, parts, medicine and orders;
- reduced fratricide and fraud;
- cross-faction standardization.

### Weaknesses

- dependent on classification and network participation;
- local blindness and refusal;
- low-confidence regions deferred;
- central failure/capture risk;
- force legitimacy tied to service success.

### Frame doctrine

- networked point defense, rapid response, infrastructure seizure and controlled technical operations.

### Failure tendency

- administrative containment replaces political solution;
- successful emergency command expands permanently.

## 19. Plural Transition Defense Doctrine

### Strategic aim

- maintain minimum defense while transferring military authority and preventing warlord or central recapture.

### Operational preferences

- regional/local defense;
- mission-specific theater command;
- shared warning and logistics;
- multi-party keys and automatic expiry;
- civilian and crew standing;
- retain limited central coordination for catastrophic threats.

### Strengths

- no single seizure controls all forces;
- local legitimacy and adaptation;
- multiple fallbacks.

### Weaknesses

- slow response;
- uneven capability;
- wealthy regions under-contribute;
- conflicting engagement rules;
- temporary commands may fail under surprise.

### Failure tendency

- local defense becomes warlordism;
- central residue expands after every crisis;
- poor regions are abandoned.

## 20. Rules of Engagement Dimensions

Every operation defines:

- enemy/hostile act classification;
- protected civilians and infrastructure;
- surrender and rescue;
- target verification confidence;
- collateral and debris limits;
- medical/Neutral zones;
- capture versus destruction priority;
- authority to escalate;
- withdrawal and mission-failure thresholds;
- evidence and after-action preservation.

Different factions may share tactical objectives but disagree over these rules.

## 21. Intelligence and Deception Doctrine

Sources:
- sensors;
- route and queue data;
- logistics/economic indicators;
- human/local intelligence;
- captured records;
- public claims;
- Aurel/Archive models.

Deception:
- decoys and emissions;
- false cargo and movement;
- conflicting lawful orders;
- forged authentication;
- selective truth release;
- hidden local alliances;
- simulated supply weakness or fleet concentration.

Fair-play:
- deception has cost, source and detectable contradiction;
- no sudden hidden fleet without prior logistics.

## 22. Battle-State Template

### Strategic context

- date/time;
- systems/nodes/routes;
- political mandate;
- civilian/service stakes;
- reinforcement and communication clocks.

### Friendly forces

For each formation:
- ships by role;
- support ships;
- frames/craft;
- personnel;
- readiness fields;
- ammunition and consumables;
- repair/yard access;
- command/ownership/withdrawal;
- morale/pay/family state.

### Opposing/third forces

- known and estimated holdings;
- source/confidence;
- objectives and constraints;
- likely reinforcements and route.

### Environment

- node windows;
- gravity/orbits/debris;
- sensors/noise;
- habitats/infrastructure;
- heat/energy limits.

### Objectives and failure

- primary/secondary;
- protected assets/people;
- capture/destruction conditions;
- retreat/surrender;
- after-action service/economic consequence.

### Persistence

After battle update:
- losses and damage;
- ammunition;
- crew fatigue/casualties;
- political and public response;
- repair time/cost;
- route/service state;
- next operation capability.

## 24. Anti-Spectacle Rules

- fleet size alone does not determine victory;
- capital ships cannot occupy communities or repair nodes;
- elite pilots cannot cover several fronts simultaneously;
- missiles and support deplete;
- crew and family/pay matter;
- destruction of infrastructure can lose the objective;
- a larger fleet has a larger logistical and political footprint;
- retreat can be successful command;
- local forces can win decisions without destroying superior fleets;
- no replacement wave erases six-year war losses.
