## 1. Core Principle

Technology does not progress on one linear power ladder.

Later systems are usually better in efficiency, safety, scale and automation, but may be worse in:

- local repairability;
- cross-faction compatibility;
- manual fallback;
- user ownership;
- operation under damaged networks;
- access without current identity or subscription;
- survival after institutional collapse.

Old technology matters when it preserves a function or interface that later systems optimized away. It is not automatically stronger.

## 2. Six Operational Layers

Every machine, node or service system can be analyzed through six layers:

1. **Physical** — structure, power, cooling, propulsion, materials.
2. **Control** — sensors, actuators, local software and pilot/operator interface.
3. **Service** — diagnostics, maintenance ports, test routines and replacement procedures.
4. **Authentication** — identity, permissions, licenses and trusted updates.
5. **Network** — communication, coordination, remote data and distributed computation.
6. **Institution** — parts, labor, training, insurance, law and responsibility.

Compatibility at one layer does not guarantee compatibility at the others.

Example:
- a Helix coolant valve may physically fit;
- its controller may require current firmware;
- the service tool may require certified access;
- the warranty may reject an open adapter;
- the operator may be legally unable to launch afterward.

## 3. Era 0 — Pre-Lattice Industrial Expansion

Working span:
- before stable interstellar lattice civilization.

Characteristics:
- robust local machinery;
- long service life;
- extensive manual controls;
- low automation and high crew/labor requirements;
- incompatible local standards;
- large physical safety margins where resources allowed.

Surviving value:
- manual fallback;
- physical repair knowledge;
- isolated habitat and mining systems;
- tools not dependent on remote authentication.

Weakness:
- inefficient, heavy, labor-intensive and dangerous without experienced crews.

Relic rule:
- Era 0 equipment is useful in isolation but rarely competitive in fleet combat or modern high-throughput infrastructure.

## 4. Era 1 — Early Relay and Rescue-Service Age

Characteristics:
- lattice relays and orbital settlements expand faster than unified standards;
- emergency service buses connect ships, habitats, tugs and utility frames;
- local operators need to repair mixed hardware at distant nodes;
- service authorities are broad, physical and role-based;
- rescue and infrastructure functions shape early maneuver-frame design.

Key inheritance:
- AUXILIA-family service architecture;
- physical authority keys and universal emergency connectors;
- 07호’s mixed lineage;
- early node maintenance layers and Authorities A–E.

Strength:
- cross-platform emergency interoperability;
- manual/local operation;
- maintainability under communication loss.

Weakness:
- poor security by later standards;
- inconsistent safety and documentation;
- broad access can be abused;
- low performance and inefficient hardware.

Misremembered history:
- later military regimes reinterpret emergency service authority as royal or strategic command authority.

## 5. Era 2 — Imperial Standardization

Characteristics:
- the expanding Empire unifies route, military, public-service and identity standards;
- command and authentication become more structured;
- mass production and trained service corps reduce local improvisation;
- AI use is bounded by office, mission and legal scope.

Strength:
- reliable large-scale logistics;
- interoperable fleets and public services;
- recognized identity, salary and command;
- better safety than fragmented early systems.

Weakness:
- local variation suppressed;
- central records become necessary for access;
- emergency service buses are hidden, narrowed or classified;
- provincial systems depend on Core updates and keys.

07 relevance:
- later military parts are layered over an older service skeleton, producing mixed response and disputed ownership.

## 6. Era 3 — Corporate Certification and Optimization

Characteristics:
- Helix and related networks improve safety, medicine, performance and lifecycle management;
- standardized testing, warranties, remote diagnostics and professional certification expand;
- systems become lighter, more efficient and more specialized;
- ownership moves from physical purchase toward service, data and update rights.

Strength:
- excellent failure statistics and medical safety;
- scalable production;
- lower crew burden;
- predictable maintenance;
- strong specialist training.

Weakness:
- proprietary lock-in;
- remote and identity dependency;
- equipment can be unusable without certified service;
- efficiency removes physical redundancy;
- customers lose control of data and modification.

Plot rule:
- certified systems must repeatedly save lives and outperform improvised systems in their supported envelope.

## 7. Era 4 — Frontier Forks and Open Service

Characteristics:
- mixed settlements adapt Imperial, Helix, industrial and legacy systems;
- workers publish interfaces, repair documentation and local standards;
- emergency compatibility is prioritized over maximum performance;
- multiple certifiers and user/worker governance emerge.

Strength:
- local adaptation;
- reduced dependency;
- broader access;
- repair under embargo or damaged routes;
- support for old and irregular assets.

Weakness:
- inconsistent materials and tests;
- counterfeit and partial implementations;
- security exposure;
- fragmented liability;
- documentation burden placed on workers.

Open Service rule:
- openness is not a technical switch. It requires test facilities, version governance, incident reporting, trainers, maintainers and accepted responsibility.

## 8. Era 5 — Continuity Seed / Aurel Integration

Characteristics:
- identity, route, medicine, pay, command and technical systems are reconciled through executable consensus;
- old and new records are ranked for current action;
- AI/human offices coordinate service restoration at enormous scale;
- systems optimize continuity across institutions rather than one machine.

Strength:
- restores broken services quickly;
- resolves incompatible current claims;
- coordinates routes and command;
- makes distributed technological ecosystems operable.

Weakness:
- classification and confidence become access;
- low-trust people and systems are deferred;
- local alternatives may be labeled unsafe;
- emergency centralization can become social order;
- one executable version hides unresolved histories.

Technology climax:
- the final conflict is not old versus new hardware, but who controls interoperability, correction and refusal.

## 9. Compatibility Classes

### C0 — Native

- designed for the same physical, control, service and authentication stack.
- highest expected performance and safety.

### C1 — Certified Adapter

- tested connection between known systems;
- limited supported configurations;
- clear liability and updates.

### C2 — Open Governed Adapter

- documented and tested by a plural/local standards body;
- known exclusions and incident reporting;
- performance may be reduced.

### C3 — Field Provisional

- current physical function demonstrated;
- incomplete safety, security or lifecycle data;
- time/mission-limited use with named stop conditions.

### C4 — Improvised Emergency

- one-use or survival-only connection;
- high monitoring and manual control;
- cannot be treated as repeatable standard.

### C5 — False Compatibility

- appears to fit or authenticate but creates hidden stress, unsafe updates, data leakage or legal lockout.

Narrative rule:
- every important cross-era integration names its class or clearly shows its equivalent risk.

## 10. Interface Families

### Physical utility interfaces

- power, coolant, structural, propellant, air/water, cargo and rescue connectors.
- easiest to see but not necessarily safest to adapt.

### Control buses

- sensor and actuator commands;
- timing mismatch can damage equipment even when physical parts fit.

### Service buses

- diagnostics, calibration, emergency isolation and maintenance.
- AUXILIA lineage is strongest here.

### Authentication layers

- pilot/operator identity;
- machine certificate;
- mission authorization;
- local facility permission;
- emergency authority.

### Data and provenance

- configuration history;
- failure records;
- maintenance and author contributions;
- patient/operator consent;
- software/model origin.

### Institutional interfaces

- who can inspect;
- who pays;
- who is liable;
- who can stop use;
- where parts and trained people come from.

## 11. Why Old Systems Survive

- replacement cost exceeds local capacity;
- old hulls and nodes have huge embedded infrastructure;
- mixed populations need compatibility;
- manual fallback is strategically valuable;
- legal ownership disputes delay replacement;
- old service interfaces remain useful;
- certain communities distrust or cannot access new authentication;
- new systems may be optimized for stable network conditions.

Old systems also persist through exploitation:
- dangerous labor;
- unavailable parts;
- energy waste;
- unequal accident rates;
- owners avoiding capital investment.

Do not romanticize old machinery.

## 12. Technology Rarity Axes

Do not use one rarity ladder.

Classify by:
- era;
- physical scarcity;
- production difficulty;
- operator scarcity;
- service-network availability;
- legal restriction;
- historical significance;
- interoperability;
- reliability;
- information completeness;
- political sensitivity.

An old service key may be common material but rare in valid authority context.
A prototype weapon may be unique but strategically useless without ammunition and support.

## 13. Mecha Development Logic

Early utility frames:
- rescue, construction, node service and cargo.

Imperial military frames:
- standardized fleet support, boarding, point capture and infrastructure defense.

Helix frames:
- efficient, safe, specialized and service-dependent.

Frontier frames:
- mixed, repairable and locally adapted, with uneven quality.

Continuity-era coordination:
- frames become nodes in a broader service/command network, increasing capability and centralization risk.

07호 progression:
- does not become strongest by receiving one final form;
- evolves through service access, mixed modules, operator governance and new public roles;
- combat performance grows but remains constrained by current body, support, heat and mission configuration;
- final significance is as a living public rescue/training/service asset, not a private legendary superweapon.

## 14. Ship and Fleet Development Logic

- new hulls improve efficiency, sensors, automation and weapon integration;
- old hulls may retain modular space, manual control and cross-standard service access;
- fleet power depends on yards, crews, ammunition, routes, authentication and political mission;
- software or command upgrades cannot repair worn structure or exhausted personnel;
- captured ships need title, crew, pay, parts, route and trust.

## 15. AI and Automation Across Eras

Era 0–1:
- task automation and local expert systems;
- human/manual fallback central.

Era 2:
- bounded office and military agents;
- legal responsibility remains with human institutions.

Era 3:
- optimized corporate models and remote service;
- proprietary data and update dependency.

Era 4:
- open/local agents with provenance and safety variation.

Era 5:
- cross-institutional reconciliation and service coordination;
- personhood and institution boundaries become central.

Rule:
- AI capability is constrained by sensors, access, compute, data, authority and physical implementation.
- no AI can create missing food, medicine, heat capacity or consent.

## 16. Failure Taxonomy

### Material failure

- wear, fracture, contamination, heat and radiation.

### Interface failure

- fit, timing, voltage, data format, control semantics.

### Authentication failure

- denied identity, expired key, conflicting authority, forged certificate.

### Update failure

- incompatible firmware/model, hidden dependency, revoked service.

### Operator failure

- fatigue, training gap, wrong assumptions, incentives and communication.

### Institutional failure

- no parts, unpaid labor, unclear liability, delayed approval, political seizure.

### Historical/provenance failure

- wrong configuration record, erased maintainer, reconstructed component treated as original.

Major incidents should combine at least two layers. Avoid one-button sabotage explanations.

## 17. Technology Acquisition and Progression Rules

A technology becomes usable when the story has secured:

- understanding;
- materials;
- tools and infrastructure;
- trained people;
- tests and stop conditions;
- authentication or lawful workaround;
- maintenance and updates;
- liability and affected-user voice.

Progression options:
- broader compatibility;
- lower maintenance;
- safer failure;
- more local control;
- better documentation;
- reduced crew burden;
- stronger performance;
- more resilient distributed operation.

Not every upgrade improves all axes.

## 18. Anti-Power-Creep Rules

- no ancient technology is universally superior;
- no future technology erases thermal, logistical or political cost;
- performance upgrades create support and signature burdens;
- interoperability can reduce maximum performance;
- safety can reduce tactical flexibility;
- openness can increase attack surface;
- central coordination can improve survival and reduce autonomy;
- manual fallback requires people and time;
- older equipment retains role through function, not nostalgia.
