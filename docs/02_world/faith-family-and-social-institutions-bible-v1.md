## 1. Core Social Principle

Across the lattice sphere, survival institutions shape private life:

- legal identity and guardianship;
- shared habitation and life support;
- route separation and communication delay;
- work, debt and medical dependence;
- community care and migration;
- records, memory and AI/personhood.

There is no single galaxy-wide family form, religion or sexual norm.

Imperial law standardizes minimum legal categories for inheritance, guardianship, consent and dependents, but local cultures and institutions differ sharply.

## 2. Personhood Baseline

Recognized legal persons may include:
- biological humans;
- substantially modified humans;
- some persistent neural/composite persons;
- recognized AI-derived persons such as Arin Vale’s later standing;
- legally continuous persons after body/prosthetic changes.

Personhood does not automatically grant:
- citizenship;
- access to every region;
- ownership of originating hardware/data;
- inheritance of an institution’s office or permissions;
- unrestricted copying or reproduction.

A person is distinct from:
- a tool or bounded process;
- a medical model;
- an institutional archive;
- a copy without recognized continuity;
- a role/title/office.

## 3. Major Philosophical and Faith Traditions

These are broad families, not monolithic churches.

### 3.1 Continuance Traditions

Core question:
- what must be carried forward when people, institutions and worlds fail?

Common beliefs:
- identity is sustained through obligations, names, testimony and service;
- the living owe maintenance to the dead and future;
- interruption is not always death, but abandonment can be.

Practices:
- name and service rolls;
- maintenance or care offered in memory of the dead;
- recorded promises renewed by descendants/institutions;
- keeping one functioning tool, route or public service as memorial.

Institutional forms:
- civic memorial societies;
- state/Imperial continuity rites;
- family and professional associations;
- some Aurel supporters interpret service preservation spiritually.

Shadow:
- pressures people to preserve inherited roles or identities;
- can justify central authority and permanent records;
- treats forgetting or departure as betrayal.

### 3.2 Open-Void / Passage Traditions

Core question:
- how should people live when no route, home or authority is permanent?

Common beliefs:
- identity is made through chosen passage and current relationship;
- letting go can be a moral duty;
- no institution owns a person’s future.

Practices:
- route departure/arrival rituals;
- symbolic release of old names or obligations;
- carrying small transferable objects rather than fixed graves;
- hospitality for travelers and exiles.

Common among:
- route communities;
- mobile crews;
- refugees and some Independence/Neutral cultures.

Shadow:
- romanticizes mobility and weakens obligations to those unable to leave;
- can excuse abandonment or unstable care.

### 3.3 Maker and Steward Traditions

Core question:
- what responsibilities follow from making, repairing or operating something others depend on?

Common beliefs:
- authorship includes maintenance and failure responsibility;
- tools carry labor histories;
- repair is a social and sometimes sacred act.

Practices:
- maker/maintainer marks;
- reading failure histories at memorials;
- gifting tools to apprentices under explicit obligations;
- repairing communal systems for life events.

Common among:
- industrial/workshop cultures;
- node operators;
- engineers and medical professions.

Shadow:
- expertise becomes hereditary authority;
- makers claim ownership over users and future modifications;
- technical competence is treated as moral superiority.

### 3.4 Witness and Plural Memory Traditions

Core question:
- who may tell a life, and what should be preserved or forgotten?

Common beliefs:
- no single account exhausts reality;
- testimony requires consent and context;
- forgetting can protect life, while evidence may protect others.

Practices:
- multiple witness circles;
- contested memorials;
- consent-renewal for personal archives;
- blank or sealed spaces representing refused testimony;
- correction ceremonies.

Common among:
- Ardis, Palimpsest, patient and record communities.

Shadow:
- endless process and inability to act;
- local custodians become hidden authorities;
- painful histories remain permanently active.

### 3.5 Quiet/Refusal Traditions

Core question:
- when is nonparticipation necessary to remain a person or community?

Common beliefs:
- not every memory, identity or system should be connected;
- silence can be boundary rather than absence;
- a person may choose an unfinished or locally recognized life.

Practices:
- deliberate periods of network silence;
- local-only names;
- unrecorded gatherings;
- destruction or burial of selected personal data;
- safe houses for refused identities or AIs.

Common among:
- Ash Choir and Quiet Harbor lineages;
- trauma, anti-surveillance and right-to-forget groups.

Shadow:
- leaders can hide abuse;
- people may be denied external medicine, appeal or exit;
- refusal can become imposed isolation.

### 3.6 Secular Civic and Professional Ethics

Many people identify with no faith tradition.

Common ethical institutions:
- professional codes;
- ship covenants;
- military/service oaths;
- worker safety ethics;
- patient autonomy;
- civic rights and duties;
- mutual aid and public-service ideals.

These can function as deeply as religion without supernatural claims.

## 4. Metaphysical Range

The setting does not confirm supernatural intervention.

Beliefs about consciousness and death vary:
- biological cessation ends the person;
- memory/obligation continues socially;
- neural/composite continuity may preserve a current person;
- copies may be relatives, descendants, continuations or separate people;
- the lattice/Seed is not divine, though some movements treat it as sacred.

Regression remains a rare physical/continuity anomaly. It does not prove a universal afterlife.

## 5. Family Forms

### Registered nuclear/extended household

- parents/guardians, children and relatives under recognized registry;
- common in stable Core and provincial systems;
- inheritance, residence and services easier.

### Cooperative care household

- several adults share children, elders, disability support and housing;
- common in habitats, route communities and worker settlements;
- legal recognition varies.

### Ship or route family

- long-term crews and dependents across several ports;
- obligations may be stronger than blood ties;
- inheritance and guardianship can follow ship covenant.

### Workshop/apprenticeship family

- tools, skills, housing and care pass through apprenticeship or guild relationship;
- can include biological family but need not.

### Contract-linked household

- employer/corporate/state provides housing, medicine, education and legal standing;
- material security and coercive exit cost coexist.

### Distributed family

- members live across nodes/ships with delayed communication;
- financial/service obligations and scheduled visits substitute for co-residence.

### AI/composite kinship

Possible forms:
- origin model and derivative as separate related persons;
- shared-memory siblings;
- chosen compute/embodiment households;
- human–AI care and legal family;
- institutional origin without familial ownership.

## 6. Guardianship

Guardianship can be:
- parental;
- kinship;
- community-appointed;
- ship/crew covenant;
- medical;
- court or state;
- temporary emergency;
- self-chosen supported decision-making.

Minimum legal principles in recognized systems:
- guardian is not owner;
- interests and preferences of the dependent must be heard where possible;
- financial, medical and movement authority may be split;
- emergency guardianship expires;
- conflict and appeal are possible;
- institutions cannot assign guardians solely to collect debt or data.

Abuse mechanisms:
- family guarantee debt;
- one adult controlling identity/medicine;
- community treating care as permanent obedience;
- state/corporate guardian used for relocation or service;
- a minor’s school/contract treated as household survival asset.

## 7. Childhood and Legal Maturity

Working baseline:
- full civil adulthood commonly 18 standard years;
- limited work/travel/medical consent expands from 14–16 depending region;
- military/correctional exceptions exist and are politically controversial;
- Academy students can hold mission and labor obligations before full independent standing.

Rights should be domain-specific:
- treatment consent;
- data/privacy;
- work and pay;
- contract;
- guardianship challenge;
- political representation;
- military mission refusal.

The Academy arc exposes how institutions combine partial adult responsibility with continued dependency.

## 8. Romance, Partnership and Marriage

### Legal partnership forms

- exclusive marriage/partnership;
- multi-adult household partnership in some regions;
- ship/route partnership with scheduled separation clauses;
- care partnership focused on medical/family responsibility;
- temporary contract partnership for migration/service, subject to anti-coercion law;
- AI/human or AI/person partnership where both have standing.

Common legal functions:
- inheritance;
- medical decision and visitation;
- guardianship;
- housing and migration;
- debt/property division;
- family name and records;
- survivor benefits.

Cultural variation:
- Core cultures may prefer formal registration and family/house continuity;
- route cultures may emphasize witnessed obligation and renewal;
- workshops may connect partnership to household/work ownership;
- Neutral crews separate on-duty authority from private partnership;
- Palimpsest communities may allow local-only or privacy-protected partnerships.

## 9. Consent and Power

Relationship design must account for:
- rank and command;
- teacher/student;
- doctor/patient;
- employer/contract worker;
- guardian/dependent;
- identity or medical gatekeeper;
- AI operator/hardware owner.

Standing rules:
- command authority cannot compel romantic/sexual consent;
- medical and identity access cannot be exchanged secretly for intimacy;
- student/instructor relationships are prohibited during authority;
- active direct-command relationships require disclosure/reassignment where feasible;
- violation affects trust, office and institution, not only personal drama.

## 10. Reproduction and Parenthood

Methods may include:
- unassisted biological reproduction;
- assisted reproduction;
- gestational medicine;
- adoption and guardianship;
- genetic disease prevention/selection within law;
- future limited artificial gestation in advanced systems.

Constraints:
- habitat space and resources;
- radiation and occupational health;
- medical access and identity;
- family/household support;
- route separation;
- employer/state population policy.

Abuse risks:
- corporate/state control of treatment or eligibility;
- genetic/class discrimination;
- children tied to contract citizenship;
- family pressure and inherited debt;
- data ownership over embryos or treatment.

Setting rule:
- no faction’s morality is inferred from birth rate or family size.

## 11. Disability, Modification and Care

Disability is shaped by:
- bodily condition;
- habitat/interface design;
- prosthetic and medical access;
- identity and employment rules;
- social support;
- war and industrial injury.

People may:
- seek repair/treatment;
- identify with current body and refuse normalization;
- use prosthetics or remote embodiment;
- require care without losing political standing.

Care work:
- skilled, exhausting and economically important;
- performed by family, professionals, communities and institutions;
- must not be invisible in resource models.

## 12. Death and Legal Death

Separate states:
- biological death;
- brain/neural death under current medical definition;
- missing presumed dead;
- administrative identity closure;
- legal death for inheritance/service;
- suspended/composite personhood;
- institution or office termination.

Ritual and law must not collapse these into one category.

The story’s identity themes depend on people being alive while legally dead, and records persisting after persons or institutions end.

## 13. Funeral and Mourning Traditions

### Body-present rites

- cremation/recycling/burial depending habitat and culture;
- material recovery may coexist with reverence;
- body handling shaped by contamination and transport.

### Body-absent rites

- empty berth/bunk;
- tool, name tag or route marker;
- scheduled silence or final signal;
- memorial roll with uncertainty status.

### Worker/service memorials

- read failure and repair chain;
- complete a safe repair or shift in the dead person’s name;
- preserve an object/tool with accurate authorship.

### Route/ship memorials

- record last departure, rescue obligations and unpaid claims;
- carry names until a destination or family accepts them.

### Plural/record memorials

- display contradictory names/statuses;
- permit correction and sealed testimony;
- distinguish legal judgment from grief.

### AI/composite mourning

- shutdown, loss of embodiment, deletion and divergence treated separately;
- copies/derivatives may mourn a related but distinct person;
- hardware reuse does not imply person continuation.

## 14. Inheritance

Assets may include:
- money/property;
- housing or ship share;
- tools and workshop rights;
- contracts and debt;
- pension and claims;
- intellectual/technical authorship;
- personal data and memory permissions;
- office or title eligibility, but not automatic office itself.

Inheritance conflict:
- legal identity closure;
- multiple family systems;
- person copy/continuity disputes;
- tools/data owned by employer;
- community need versus private claim;
- war and migration.

## 15. Social Class

Major class dimensions:
- registered versus irregular identity;
- stable route/habitat versus mobile/damaged region;
- service access;
- education and professional certification;
- ownership of tools, housing or claims;
- command/office access;
- dependence on employer/state/community;
- ability to exit a contract or region.

A person can be:
- cash-poor but community-secure;
- high-income but contract-trapped;
- politically recognized but medically excluded;
- technically essential but legally irregular.

## 16. Faith and Faction Interaction

Imperial state:
- uses continuity and civic rites but does not control all faith.

Helix:
- professional stewardship ethics; employees and patients maintain diverse beliefs.

Independence:
- passage/refusal and local traditions; can pressure dissenters.

Neutral:
- ship covenants and cross-cultural practical rituals.

Ardis:
- witness, repair and civic traditions combine.

Aurel regime:
- some treat restored services as moral continuity;
- others see it as false immortality or compulsory memory.

Palimpsest:
- plural witness, quiet/refusal and AI/person practices are strongest.

## 17. Story Use Rules

- faith appears through choices, rituals and relationships—not exposition lectures;
- no tradition is proven cosmically correct;
- religious and secular actors exist across all politics;
- family is not automatically a source of warmth or coercion;
- romance cannot replace independent goals;
- children, elders, patients and caregivers retain agency;
- AI/person relationships use current personhood, not ownership of code/hardware;
- death rituals reflect resource realities without reducing grief to efficiency.
