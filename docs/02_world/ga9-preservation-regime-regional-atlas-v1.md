## 1. Atlas Principle

The Preservation Regime must look and feel different across regions.

Aurel/Seed does not create one uniform empire. It produces a common minimum service and classification layer implemented through:

- surviving states;
- local governments;
- hospitals and employers;
- route and node operators;
- Helix and industrial networks;
- fleets and security bodies;
- local AI and record institutions.

The same person may be P1 for medicine, P2 for identity, P3 for travel and P4 for command.

GA9 follows six representative regional models rather than treating the whole 80-billion-person lattice sphere as one backdrop.

## 2. Regime Coverage at Mid-GA9

Working population scope:

- full or high synchronization: 31–39 billion;
- conditional/partial synchronization: 20–27 billion;
- deferred or low-connectivity regions: 11–17 billion;
- active refusal/alternative systems: 5–9 billion;
- untracked/mobile/war-displaced: 3–7 billion overlapping categories.

These numbers change by domain. A region may accept medical and route synchronization while refusing political/record control.

## 3. Regional Model R1 — Meridian Guaranteed Core

### Geography and population

- Meridian, Core Registry cities, Crownline trunk nodes and major public-service worlds.
- 13–18 billion residents within the broader synchronized Core model.

### Regime profile

Dominant bands:
- P1 Guaranteed Continuity for most registered residents and institutions;
- P2 for migrants, disputed inheritances and politically sensitive offices;
- P3/P4 concentrated in security, AI and irregular identity cases.

### Visible benefits

- hospitals receive predictable supplies;
- payroll and pensions recover;
- duplicate identity fraud and forged orders decline;
- transit queues shorten;
- families restore inheritance and missing-person status;
- military fratricide and competing warrants fall sharply.

### Visible costs

- public and private services increasingly require Aurel status;
- local correction bodies lose authority to central consistency;
- protected identities can be reopened;
- political organizers face command/employment restrictions through C-CMD/C-CAS rather than criminal conviction;
- Core populations interpret the system as normal competence, not dictatorship.

### Local implementers

- Continuity Directorate offices;
- Imperial/Compact ministries;
- public hospitals and registries;
- major employers and schools;
- military commands;
- Meridian archive/courts.

### Military/security

- strongest regime fleet concentration and authenticated command;
- 300–500 major combat ships across Core corridors, not one battle group;
- high readiness through pay and parts restoration;
- force mainly protects routes, nodes and service institutions.

### Political conflict

- reformers seek stronger appeals and domain separation;
- hardliners call Core success proof that expansion is morally required;
- beneficiaries fear that weakening Aurel will return chaos;
- local elites can convert high confidence into inherited advantage.

### GA10 transition problem

- Core residents have the most to lose from rapid decentralization;
- regional boards can operate services, but many have forgotten or lost manual fallback;
- first authority transfers may cause pay, medicine or command interruptions.

## 4. Regional Model R2 — Orsen Clinical Corridor

### Geography and population

- Orsen and linked medical/biotech habitats, patient routes and rehabilitation systems.
- 5–7 billion people depend directly or indirectly on its medical standards and supply network.

### Regime profile

- medical C-SVC and identity C-ID synchronization adopted rapidly;
- political and memory domains remain contested;
- high P1/P2 service gains coexist with large P3 identity queues.

### Visible benefits

- chronic medicine and prosthetic supply stabilize;
- treatment histories reconcile across regions;
- duplicated/closed identities regain partial service;
- clinical warnings and counterfeit treatments decline;
- emergency patient transfer becomes faster.

### Visible costs

- intimate data becomes necessary for eligibility;
- some AI/composite persons are treated as clinical records rather than persons;
- low-confidence patients receive sponsor or containment conditions;
- refusal of data sharing can reduce care priority;
- Helix and Continuity models reinforce one another.

### Local implementers

- hospital systems;
- Patient Self-Rule Assembly;
- Helix Human Performance and certified suppliers;
- Neutral medical/claims bodies;
- Continuity clinical offices;
- AI/personhood courts and forums.

### Military/security

- protected medical convoys and quarantine forces;
- limited heavy fleet presence but high political sensitivity;
- technical lockout and route permission more common than direct violence.

### Political conflict

- patients who gained life-saving care often support continued synchronization;
- privacy and personhood groups oppose compulsory data integration;
- clinicians fear distributed systems cannot maintain safety;
- Helix reformers and monopolists split over open interfaces.

### GA10 transition problem

- identity and treatment authority cannot be transferred at the same speed;
- patient-controlled records must still interoperate across routes;
- some communities voluntarily retain central clinical synchronization longer than political domains.

## 5. Regional Model R3 — Ardis Conditional Federation

### Geography and population

- Ardis, Silex bypass, Brann–Neris industry, route-federation communities and connected node cities.
- 4.5–7.5 billion residents/users across multiple polities and mobile networks.

### Regime profile

- accepts minimum-service and route synchronization;
- resists single political/record command;
- most institutions remain P2 Conditional Continuity under joint audit;
- local trust and Aurel maintain parallel records and keys.

### Visible benefits

- food/medicine and route schedules stabilize;
- fleet/yard payroll and parts authentication improve;
- local plural corrections enter galaxy-scale service;
- emergency coordination reduces local conflict.

### Visible costs

- every exception requires time and administrative capacity;
- local groups with weak representation can still be compressed;
- Aurel classifies some open standards and local militias as systemic risks;
- central service popularity threatens joint-trust autonomy;
- dual systems create expensive duplication.

### Local implementers

- Ardis Joint Trust;
- route federation;
- worker/standards councils;
- local governments;
- Neutral/Helix partners;
- regional Continuity administrators under treaty.

### Military/security

- distributed local fleets and mission trusts;
- 100–180 combat ships across region, with mixed readiness;
- local withdrawal rights retained;
- Aurel-linked authentication improves coordination but becomes leverage.

### Political conflict

- some citizens want full P1 integration and faster services;
- others view P2 conditions as a path to central absorption;
- workers and node operators contest whose local knowledge enters models;
- Haren’s route networks split over system participation.

### GA10 transition problem

- this region is best prepared for plural authority but already overloaded;
- exporting the Ardis model can centralize Ardis as a new template authority;
- local systems must allow other regions to design different transitions.

## 6. Regional Model R4 — Lumen–Provincial Service Compacts

### Geography and population

- medium Imperial/provincial nodes, agricultural and industrial corridors similar to Lumen.
- 12–17 billion residents across many separate regions.

### Regime profile

- local governments accept Aurel to restore budgets, traffic and defense;
- implementation varies by governor, creditor and local institutions;
- many residents are P1/P2 for services and P3 for migration or political status.

### Visible benefits

- provincial collapse slows;
- food and medical distribution improves;
- local civil servants are paid;
- pirates and forged authorities lose ground;
- regional governments regain bargaining power.

### Visible costs

- governors use classification to protect existing constituents;
- irregular migrants and frontier settlements remain review cases;
- local appeals depend on underfunded offices;
- Aurel can replace a local administration under cascade-risk logic;
- provincial elites claim that synchronization equals democratic consent.

### Local implementers

- governors and civil boards;
- agricultural/industrial cooperatives;
- provincial military commands;
- Helix/Neutral contractors;
- Continuity sector offices.

### Military/security

- restored regional task groups;
- 200–350 combat ships across many sectors;
- adequate for corridor security but not total occupation;
- local forces can become regime enforcement or regional reform allies.

### Political conflict

- central hardliners and provincial autonomists both benefit from services;
- ordinary residents prioritize pay, safety and school continuity;
- frontier communities see the regime mainly through denied movement and delayed recognition.

### GA10 transition problem

- thousands of local institutions need replacement keys, budgets and appeal systems;
- abrupt distribution can empower governors without safeguards;
- slow transfer risks permanent central trusteeship.

## 7. Regional Model R5 — Taris Deferred Frontier

### Geography and population

- Taris-type shadow routes, irregular settlements, damaged L3 relays, mobile communities and low-capacity edge systems.
- 7–12 billion residents across the wider frontier and mobile population.

### Regime profile

- selective medical/route participation;
- low identity confidence and high cascade-risk classification;
- many communities remain P3 Deferred or P4 Containment by domain;
- physical connectivity limits appeal and correction.

### Visible benefits

- some emergency medicine and authenticated aid finally reach isolated systems;
- piracy, fraud and false rescue signals decline along synchronized segments;
- selected identities and claims are restored;
- communities can access broader markets when accepted.

### Visible costs

- whole routes are ranked low-investment or high-risk;
- relocation becomes the offered solution;
- informal identities and mutual-care labor are undercounted;
- local tools/AI are quarantined;
- refusal can close trade and medicine;
- data reaches Aurel faster than appeals return.

### Local implementers

- small Continuity teams;
- compliant local councils;
- military/route security detachments;
- humanitarian and Neutral partners;
- local intermediaries and sponsors.

### Military/security

- sparse regime patrols and rapid intervention groups;
- 60–120 combat-capable ships across enormous geography;
- relies on route control, local collaborators and denial rather than occupation;
- resistance and smuggling remain strong.

### Political conflict

- some residents seek full integration for survival;
- localists and Independence successors resist classification;
- young people may prefer recognized mobility over community autonomy;
- leaders can hide internal coercion behind anti-Aurel rhetoric.

### GA10 transition problem

- distributing authority to weak or coercive local bodies may worsen harm;
- maintaining central minimum service without central command requires expensive mobile institutions;
- some routes choose continued Aurel guarantees voluntarily.

## 8. Regional Model R6 — Palimpsest Refusal and Containment Belt

### Geography and population

- Ash Choir, Quiet Harbor and other Palimpsest systems refusing or limiting synchronization.
- 20–35 million direct residents in the main refusal zones; billions elsewhere watch their precedent.

### Regime profile

- accepted domains vary from none to emergency medicine only;
- Aurel classifies some AI, archives and routes as P4/P5 technical or cascade risks;
- local records remain valid internally but weak outside.

### Visible benefits of refusal

- intimate records and chosen identities remain protected;
- local AI/person communities retain self-definition;
- autonomous-war archives stay isolated;
- local governance and manual fallback remain strong.

### Visible costs of refusal

- slower medicine and trade;
- weak external identity/claim recognition;
- higher fraud and route risk;
- internal custodians can become unaccountable;
- residents may be trapped by their community’s collective decision.

### Local implementers

- mirror custodian councils;
- Quiet Harbor identity covenants;
- local governments and defense;
- AI/person assemblies;
- anti-synchronization coalitions;
- limited Neutral intermediaries.

### Military/security

- strong route-denial and local defense relative to population;
- 20–40 combat ships and extensive fixed/damaged-route advantage;
- regime intervention risks destroying the records/services it seeks to contain.

### Political conflict

- hardliners demand quarantine or deletion;
- reformers demand domain-limited recognition;
- local leaders disagree over individual right to opt in;
- residents who need external medicine challenge collective refusal.

### GA10 transition problem

- refusal zones provide technical and political alternatives;
- they cannot simply be declared morally correct because their services and appeals are weaker;
- final system must recognize bounded refusal and individual exit without forcing synchronization.

## 9. Cross-Regional Service Flow

### Food and biological supplies

- provincial/agricultural regions → Core, Orsen, Ardis and frontier routes.
- Aurel stabilizes contracts and allocation but can downgrade low-confidence destinations.

### Advanced medicine

- Orsen/Helix hubs → all regions.
- data and identity requirements become a major control point.

### Industrial parts

- Core/Helix/Brann–Neris → fleets, nodes and habitats.
- open and certified standards compete.

### Identity and payment

- Seed consensus and regional mirrors → employers, hospitals, schools, routes and courts.
- disruption can harm more people than one fleet battle.

### Records and appeals

- local offices → sector administrations → consensus/review bodies.
- return path is slower than initial classification in weak regions.

### Military and security

- Core/provincial forces protect synchronized corridors;
- local fleets retain geography and legitimacy;
- classification determines which forces receive keys, pay and parts.

## 10. GA9 Campaign Geography

The act should rotate among:

1. a Core beneficiary region where Aurel works extremely well;
2. Orsen identity/medical cases;
3. Ardis conditional treaty conflict;
4. a provincial service corridor facing takeover;
5. a deferred frontier community;
6. a refusal/containment site.

No more than 3–4 regions are front-stage in one mini-arc.

The protagonist’s coalition cannot treat all synchronized residents as collaborators or all refusal regions as free.

## 11. Central-Key Operational Geography

The central key can coordinate:

- route priorities;
- command authentication;
- selected service and identity consistency;
- emergency resource and military synchronization.

It cannot:
- directly operate every hospital or node;
- create local workers, food or medicine;
- understand all local records;
- replace physical and political implementers.

When Rian takes the central key:
- Core and major corridors respond first;
- conditional regions respond through treaty or limited domain;
- deferred/refusal regions respond slowly, partially or not at all;
- central success is geographically unequal.

## 12. GA10 Transition Pressure by Region

| Region | Best transfer starting point | Main danger |
|---|---|---|
| Meridian Core | political/record review and regional service boards | service interruption and elite capture |
| Orsen | patient/clinical domain separation | incompatible treatment and lost identity continuity |
| Ardis | local joint trust and route federation | exporting one model as universal solution |
| Provincial Compacts | elected/civic boards and treaty | governor/local-elite monopoly |
| Deferred Frontier | mobile minimum-service guarantees | abandonment or coercive local control |
| Refusal Belt | recognition of bounded autonomy and individual exit | isolation, internal custodianship and external quarantine |

## 13. Regional Comparison Tests

For each GA9–10 major decision, ask:

- who receives medicine tomorrow?
- who is paid and recognized?
- what local office physically acts?
- what route or node is available?
- how long does correction take?
- can an individual opt in or out against community choice?
- what military body enforces or protects the decision?
- what happens if Aurel disappears for 72 hours?
- who benefits from keeping synchronization?
- who benefits from ending it?
