## 1. Atlas Principle

GA10 does not divide the galaxy into “free” and “controlled” territory.

Each representative region transfers seven functions at different speeds:

1. identity/legal status;
2. minimum services;
3. routes/nodes;
4. military command;
5. technical/AI/industrial authority;
6. records/memory;
7. emergency corrective power.

A region can improve in one domain while failing in another.

The final order is plural only if:
- regional choices are real;
- dependent outsiders have standing;
- individuals can appeal or leave;
- minimum interoperability survives;
- no permanent master key remains;
- transition harms are named and compensated rather than hidden.

## 2. Common Service-State Scale

For each function, track:

- `S0 Collapsed` — no reliable institution or service;
- `S1 Emergency` — temporary external/central operation;
- `S2 Conditional` — functioning with restrictive or unstable terms;
- `S3 Regional` — locally governed with cross-region recognition;
- `S4 Federated` — reciprocal multi-region operation and appeal;
- `S5 Centralized` — highly reliable central operation with limited local control.

The goal is not moving every function to S4. Some regions voluntarily retain S5-like synchronization in bounded domains.

## 3. TR-1 — Ardis Immediate Plural Transfer

### Population and geography

- Ardis/Silex/Brann–Neris and connected route communities;
- 5–8 billion people affected directly or through logistics and service agreements.

### Starting state

- identity: S4/S5 hybrid;
- minimum service: S4 with central reserves;
- routes: S4 local/federated plus central authentication;
- military: S3/S4 mission trusts;
- technology: S4 plural standards;
- records: S4 plural mirrors;
- corrective power: S5 central residue.

### Transition plan

1. transfer regional current-status issuance to joint trust and partner polities;
2. keep cross-region provisional recognition;
3. move route authority to local custodians plus federation/Neutral audit;
4. split central fleet keys into mission trusts;
5. retain central disaster reserves temporarily;
6. distribute Authority G into event-specific multi-party panels.

### Material strengths

- trained local operators;
- plural records;
- worker and user institutions;
- independent fleets and yards;
- experience with shared keys and emergency charters.

### Material weaknesses

- expensive duplication;
- slow decision;
- local technical elites and institutions already powerful;
- other regions depend on Ardis transit and standards;
- risk of turning Ardis into a new central model-provider.

### Transition conflict

- local blocs disagree whether to accept external dependents and costs;
- some citizens prefer Aurel’s faster service;
- rivals sabotage the first local identity and route handoff;
- central hardliners exploit predictable delay.

### Required failure

- first identity or route transfer creates duplicate/denied cases and a preventable service loss;
- local institutions correct it publicly rather than asking central authority to conceal it.

### End state

- most functions S3/S4;
- central reserve and translation remain bounded;
- Ardis has no power to command other regions;
- local opposition and inequality persist.

## 4. TR-2 — Meridian Hybrid Synchronized Autonomy

### Population and geography

- Core cities, major state institutions and trunk routes;
- 13–18 billion residents in the representative model.

### Starting state

- identity/services/routes/command: S5;
- technology and records: S4/S5;
- local political control: constrained by Continuity emergency.

### Transition plan

1. transfer political and record-review authority first;
2. regionalize hospitals, payroll and identity operations only after capacity tests;
3. split military command by theater/mission;
4. maintain high synchronization for medicine and payment under fixed term;
5. create elected/independent oversight and hard expiration for central corrective power.

### Strengths

- large professional civil service;
- wealthy service institutions;
- strong infrastructure and reserves;
- established courts and archives.

### Weaknesses

- local systems depend on Aurel interfaces;
- elite offices can capture regional authority;
- population fears service collapse;
- manual fallback atrophied;
- central beneficiaries oppose rapid transfer.

### Transition conflict

- the majority may vote to keep centralized service longer;
- reformers want political authority separated immediately;
- hardliners provoke failures to prove decentralization unsafe;
- regions outside Core demand redistribution of reserves and infrastructure.

### Required central benefit

- one central intervention prevents a major payroll/medical cascade during transition.

### Required distributed benefit

- local review reverses wrongful classification the central system would have maintained for consistency.

### End state

- political, record and military powers regionalized;
- medical/payment synchronization retained voluntarily under renewable but nonpermanent compact;
- no Core office holds universal master authority.

## 5. TR-3 — Orsen Patient-Governed Clinical Transition

### Population and geography

- Orsen medical corridor and client regions;
- 5–7 billion service dependents.

### Starting state

- medical and identity: S5;
- patient/personhood governance: S2/S3;
- technical supply: Helix/Aurel hybrid;
- records: S3–S5 depending institution.

### Transition plan

1. separate treatment continuity from political identity ranking;
2. create patient-controlled current-status tokens recognized across regions;
3. distribute clinical certification among public, Helix, Neutral and patient bodies;
4. preserve cross-region emergency data while minimizing intimate history;
5. recognize AI/composite persons independently from clinical files;
6. maintain bounded central epidemic/catastrophic-risk coordination.

### Strengths

- advanced hospitals and professional networks;
- strong claims/consent institutions;
- clear evidence of central medical benefit;
- patient and AI-rights movements.

### Weaknesses

- proprietary supplies and models;
- treatment tied to identity and data;
- unequal regional capacity;
- difficult cross-system safety certification;
- patients cannot tolerate long experiments in governance.

### Transition conflict

- central and Helix clinicians warn that plural systems will kill people;
- patients demand current treatment without permanent surveillance;
- some AI systems seek person status while others remain medical tools;
- poor regions cannot fund local clinical authority.

### Required failure

- a distributed clinical handoff loses or delays a small but real set of treatment updates;
- the answer is not full recentralization but better reciprocal protocol and compensation.

### End state

- treatment interoperability S4;
- identity/personhood authority S3/S4;
- central catastrophic-health role retained with split trigger and sunset;
- some hospitals voluntarily maintain deeper synchronization.

## 6. TR-4 — Lumen Provincial Treaty Transition

### Population and geography

- many medium corridors and provincial states;
- 12–17 billion in the model.

### Starting state

- services/routes: S4/S5;
- politics and records: S2/S3 under central/provincial mixture;
- military: S4 with central authentication;
- local appeal: uneven.

### Transition plan

1. sign region-specific transition treaties rather than one universal schedule;
2. divide authority among elected/civic boards, technical operators and external guarantors;
3. transfer fiscal and service budgets with transparency requirements;
4. split provincial military keys and require mission mandates;
5. create mobile appeal and audit for weak local offices;
6. retain interregional reserve contribution.

### Strengths

- functioning local administrations;
- agricultural/industrial base;
- regional fleets and civil services;
- existing identity and court structures.

### Weaknesses

- governors and creditors can capture authority;
- irregular settlements lack representation;
- thousands of local systems vary greatly;
- central systems currently equalize some poor regions.

### Transition conflict

- provincial leaders demand full transfer of assets and force;
- local minorities demand central protection;
- wealthy corridors resist burden-sharing;
- military commands disagree over civilian authority.

### Required failure

- one provincial government uses transferred status/route power to exclude rivals or migrants;
- external appeal and local opposition must correct it without recreating indefinite central rule.

### End state

- identity/services/routes mostly S3/S4;
- military mission command S3/S4;
- central equalization/reserve body survives with limited fiscal mandate;
- provincial capture remains a recurring danger.

## 7. TR-5 — Taris Mobile Minimum-Service Transition

### Population and geography

- shadow routes, damaged frontier systems, mobile communities and irregular settlements;
- 7–12 billion across the broad frontier model.

### Starting state

- local politics: S2/S3;
- central services: S1–S3;
- identity/route recognition: uneven S1–S3;
- military/security: local and fragmented;
- records and technology: heterogeneous.

### Transition plan

1. deploy mobile identity, medical, claims and route teams not controlled by one local faction;
2. recognize community and person records provisionally across borders;
3. fund manual/offline service fallback;
4. create local route federations with individual opt-in/exit;
5. separate humanitarian minimums from military compliance;
6. maintain temporary central/Neutral guarantees until local capacity exists.

### Strengths

- local adaptability;
- route knowledge;
- manual fallback;
- resistance to one central seizure;
- mobile communities and mutual aid.

### Weaknesses

- weak medicine, finance and courts;
- coercive local leaders;
- unreliable records;
- low defense and repair capacity;
- long travel/communication delays.

### Transition conflict

- localists call external minimum-service teams a new occupation;
- individuals seek recognition against community decisions;
- central hardliners use piracy and fraud as evidence for permanent control;
- wealthy regions resist paying for mobile services.

### Required choice

- some communities voluntarily retain Aurel-linked route/identity service while rejecting political command;
- others choose riskier local systems.

### End state

- identity and minimum services S2–S4 depending route;
- no universal frontier government;
- cross-region compact funds mobile guarantees and appeal;
- inequality remains visible.

## 8. TR-6 — Palimpsest Opt-Out Federation

### Population and geography

- Ash Choir, Quiet Harbor, Loom and related mirror systems;
- 20–35 million primary residents plus external AI/person and archival constituencies.

### Starting state

- local records/personhood: S3/S4;
- external recognition: S1/S2;
- central route/medical participation: selective;
- corrective power: local, fragmented and defensive.

### Transition plan

1. recognize bounded opt-out by domain;
2. guarantee individual right to leave, join external service or preserve local identity;
3. create translation without compulsory synchronization;
4. maintain quarantine only for demonstrated technical hazards with appeal;
5. preserve isolated autonomous-war archives;
6. allow local AI/person governments and nonterritorial communities standing.

### Strengths

- strong source and consent practices;
- manual/local technical capacity;
- alternative AI/personhood institutions;
- proven resistance to centralized record capture.

### Weaknesses

- weaker external services;
- local custodians can dominate;
- fragmented defense and finance;
- inability to prove identity/claims outside;
- internal conflict over people who want synchronization.

### Transition conflict

- central/neighbor regions fear dangerous unsynchronized technology and identity fraud;
- local councils fear any shared protocol becomes a new master system;
- individual patients and migrants challenge collective refusal.

### Required failure

- one opt-out community suppresses an individual’s right to access outside medicine or identity;
- plural order must protect the person without annexing the community.

### End state

- bounded domain opt-out recognized;
- cross-region provisional identity, rescue and claims available;
- no compulsory Archive/Seed synchronization;
- dangerous systems remain subject to narrow, reviewable containment.

## 9. TR-7 — Collapsed and Coerced Corridor Trust

### Population and geography

- war-damaged regions, occupied nodes, failed local states and contested refugee corridors;
- 1–4 billion people across many disconnected cases.

### Starting state

- most functions S0/S1;
- central force or local warlords may control one service;
- no uncontested representation.

### Transition plan

1. maintain food, medicine, rescue and identity through temporary mission trusts;
2. separate security from political succession;
3. create district/community representation and monitored elections/appointments;
4. train operators and civil workers;
5. transfer one function at a time;
6. publish exit criteria for external trustees;
7. prevent local/central actors from treating aid as permanent ownership.

### Strengths

- outside support and central capacity can stop immediate collapse;
- displaced communities often have strong informal networks;
- mission trusts and Neutral/route institutions provide temporary legitimacy.

### Weaknesses

- violence and scarcity;
- weak records and skilled workforce;
- competing armed claims;
- high risk of proxy rule;
- local institutions may be corrupt or exclusionary.

### Transition conflict

- immediate withdrawal causes death and warlord capture;
- prolonged trusteeship recreates Preservation rule;
- external factions sponsor competing authorities;
- residents disagree whether stability or sovereignty comes first.

### Required ambiguity

- some regions require central or external control longer than the protagonists want;
- no fixed date can guarantee readiness;
- refusal of permanent rule must coexist with material non-abandonment.

### End state

- mixed S1–S3;
- no claim that every region reaches plural stability by episode 1100;
- epilogue shows continuing trusts, disputes and reconstruction.

## 10. Nonterritorial Transition — AI and Composite Communities

These communities may exist:
- in distributed compute;
- across ships/habitats;
- embodied in one system;
- as current persons derived from institutional models;
- inside medical or archival infrastructures.

Required agreements:
- compute, energy and embodiment rights;
- copy/derivative/current-person distinction;
- movement and service access;
- data and intimate memory control;
- work and political representation;
- shutdown, succession and fork rules;
- cross-region recognition.

A territorial government cannot simply own an AI person because it owns the hardware.
An AI person cannot claim all institutional records or permissions from which it emerged.

Arin Vale’s final role:
- one independent person and witness to the transition;
- not successor to Aurel or universal representative of AI life.

## 11. Transition War Fronts

GA10 conflicts should map to functions and regions:

1. Core service continuity and political handoff;
2. Orsen patient/AI identity transition;
3. Ardis route and command distribution;
4. provincial capture and appeal;
5. frontier mobile service and security;
6. Palimpsest opt-out and individual exit;
7. collapsed corridor liberation/trusteeship;
8. central key and interregional reserve.

Rian can intervene centrally but cannot personally lead every regional transfer.

## 12. Central Residue Map

Functions allowed to survive centrally in bounded form:

- disaster modelling and early warning;
- interregional reserve coordination;
- translation and provisional recognition;
- catastrophic-risk technical alerts;
- temporary theater/route coordination under multi-party trigger;
- cross-region claims and audit support.

Functions not allowed to remain permanently central:

- final identity status;
- universal route permission;
- standing fleet command;
- one technical certification monopoly;
- one canonical history;
- unilateral deletion/containment;
- exclusive emergency corrective power.

## 13. Transition Metrics

Track per region and domain:

- minimum-service uptime;
- preventable mortality and treatment interruption;
- identity/appeal resolution time;
- route safety and closure;
- military response and civilian control;
- local participation and individual exit;
- technical incident rate;
- central override frequency and duration;
- wealth/service inequality;
- compensation and correction completion;
- number of authority layers still dependent on one key/person/institution.

No single metric defines success.

## 14. Epilogue State Samples

### Three years later

- Core medicine and payment still deeply synchronized but politically controlled by regional compact;
- Ardis and several federations operate plural route/identity systems;
- frontier mobile services remain uneven;
- Palimpsest preserves opt-out domains;
- some collapsed corridors remain under temporary trust;
- central override has been invoked in limited disasters and publicly audited;
- service inequality and political disputes remain;
- no permanent master key exists;
- Rian has no authority to reorder the system by personal index.
