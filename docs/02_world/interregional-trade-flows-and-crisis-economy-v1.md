## 1. Purpose

This document turns resources into causal geography.

A trade link matters when its failure changes:
- food and water;
- medicine and identity;
- heat and power;
- industrial repair;
- fleet readiness;
- movement and refuge;
- employment, pay and political allegiance.

No region is fully self-sufficient in all strategic goods.

## 2. Trade Network Tiers

### Tier A — Trunk Settlement and High-Value Freight

Moves:
- payment/claims;
- advanced medicine;
- electronics and models;
- official records and keys;
- high-priority passengers;
- military and industrial components.

Traits:
- high value, low mass;
- dependent on authentication, insurance and route priority;
- disruption is politically immediate.

### Tier B — Regional Industrial Freight

Moves:
- standard parts;
- machine tools;
- medical consumables;
- food cultures;
- ammunition;
- replacement crews and specialists.

Traits:
- medium mass/value;
- regular schedules and yard/warehouse networks;
- delayed failure appears after days/weeks.

### Tier C — Bulk Survival Freight

Moves:
- food biomass;
- water and volatiles;
- reaction mass;
- structural materials;
- thermal storage materials;
- fertilizer and waste streams.

Traits:
- huge mass and low unit value;
- vulnerable to transport capacity and orbital position;
- cannot be replaced by money or small smugglers at scale.

### Tier D — Living and Institutional Flows

Moves:
- workers;
- clinicians;
- operators;
- families and refugees;
- teachers, archivists and administrators;
- professional licenses and institutional practices.

Traits:
- slow to replace;
- movement changes sending and receiving communities;
- loyalty, housing, schooling and family rights matter.

## 3. Core Economic Regions

### Meridian/Core Registry Sphere

Exports:
- finance and settlement;
- public-service credentials;
- advanced electronics;
- administrative/technical specialists;
- military equipment and high-grade manufacturing.

Imports:
- food biomass;
- water/volatiles;
- industrial materials;
- labor and regional goods.

Critical dependencies:
- trunk nodes;
- provincial food and bulk routes;
- institutional trust in IRC and state claims.

Failure pattern:
- financial and command disruption spreads fast;
- physical scarcity appears later because of reserves;
- political response may prioritize continuity over peripheral shortages.

### Orsen Medical Corridor

Exports:
- pharmaceuticals;
- prosthetics and neural treatment;
- clinical models and diagnostics;
- medical training and specialist teams.

Imports:
- sterile materials;
- biological cultures;
- energy and cooling;
- advanced electronics;
- food and patient support.

Critical dependencies:
- identity/treatment records;
- Helix/public supply chains;
- cold and sterile transport.

Failure pattern:
- treatment interruption harms current patients before market prices adjust;
- hospitals ration by eligibility and prognosis;
- black markets cannot safely replace full clinical systems.

### Lumen/Vela Provincial Corridor

Exports:
- food;
- biological materials;
- provincial labor;
- medium industrial goods;
- route and customs services.

Imports:
- advanced medicine;
- high-grade components;
- credit and public-service authentication;
- military equipment.

Critical dependencies:
- Lumen Gate and provincial administration;
- agricultural energy/water/fertilizer;
- Core and Helix recognition.

Failure pattern:
- food can exist locally while export contracts and local pay collapse;
- central requisition creates local resentment and hoarding.

### Brann–Neris–Ardis Industrial Federation

Exports:
- repairs;
- custom and standard parts;
- utility/military frames;
- ship modules;
- open/compatible standards;
- route and node service.

Imports:
- food;
- advanced electronics;
- medical supplies;
- finance/insurance;
- raw materials and fuel.

Critical dependencies:
- skilled workers;
- machine tools and clean manufacturing;
- route access and legal recognition;
- energy/thermal capacity.

Failure pattern:
- equipment exists but production stops when workers, tools or power fail;
- sanctions produce unsafe substitutes and worker migration.

### Kael/Neutral Free Routes

Exports:
- arbitration;
- insurance;
- transport and rescue;
- crew and ship services;
- evidence/claims trust;
- mixed-market access.

Imports:
- all physical goods consumed by transient populations and fleets.

Critical dependencies:
- reputation and member cooperation;
- route safety;
- capital reserves;
- port capacity.

Failure pattern:
- members withdraw separately;
- insurance refusal can stop technically possible movement;
- humanitarian and merchant obligations split.

### Taris/Lysa Frontier Networks

Exports:
- local resources;
- hidden/alternate routes;
- salvage;
- low-cost labor and local knowledge;
- political sanctuary.

Imports:
- medicine;
- certified parts;
- food during local failure;
- legal identity and external credit;
- skilled specialists.

Critical dependencies:
- informal trust and small carriers;
- local life support;
- access to one or two recognized markets.

Failure pattern:
- communities fragment or migrate;
- cash loses value before mutual obligations do;
- military/political cells can seize transport.

### Palimpsest Reach

Exports:
- archive indices;
- translation and emulation;
- legacy maintenance;
- provenance and old route references;
- specialized AI/model knowledge.

Imports:
- medicine;
- modern parts;
- food and energy for some habitats;
- external recognition and transport.

Critical dependencies:
- low-capacity routes;
- specialist maintainers;
- local consent and archive integrity.

Failure pattern:
- outside demand raises local costs and threatens privacy;
- technical knowledge cannot immediately scale into mass production.

## 5. Lumen Corridor Flow Example

Normal monthly regional pattern — design ranges:

- Vela and related farms export 180,000–300,000 person-months of food equivalents;
- Lumen redistributes Core/Orsen medicine and finance;
- Brann/Neris export parts and repair hours;
- Kael handles claims, crews and irregular cargo;
- K-13/White Dock supply training, service and local repair;
- Doran/Lysa/Taris depend on small-route transport and mixed recognition.

A Lumen closure causes:

Day 0–3:
- route queues and settlement uncertainty;
- price jumps in priority transport, medicine and parts.

Day 4–10:
- Vela export/storage bottleneck;
- Doran/Lysa shortages;
- Brann/Neris production delays;
- military/customs requisition.

Day 11–30:
- layoffs and unpaid workers;
- treatment interruption;
- food spoilage in producing regions and hunger in consuming regions;
- smuggling and Independence route expansion;
- Silex/Ardis bypass value rises.

## 6. Fleet Concentration Economic Effect

A task group of:
- 20 combat ships;
- 10 support ships;
- 8,000–20,000 personnel;
- 50–150 frames/craft

can consume or reserve:
- a small node’s berth and repair capacity;
- thousands of person-months of food/water/life support;
- major propellant and thermal stocks;
- local medical and housing capacity;
- route priority that delays civilian trade.

Effects:
- jobs and demand increase;
- local prices and rents rise;
- military requisition and security restrictions expand;
- civilians lose route and hospital access;
- suppliers gain influence;
- withdrawal can collapse local businesses afterward.

Large fleet movement must appear economically before the battle.

## 7. Refugee and Evacuation Economy

People require:
- transport space;
- food/water/air;
- medical and sanitation;
- identity and destination acceptance;
- housing, work, education and care after arrival.

Immediate transport capacity is not successful resettlement.

Typical first-90-day cost in stable receiving region:
- basic adult: 120–240 BSC;
- child: 90–180;
- medically dependent person: 250–2,000+;
- housing construction or long-term treatment can greatly exceed this.

Host-region effects:
- labor supply and demand;
- rent and service pressure;
- cultural/political change;
- external subsidies and debt;
- local resentment and new institutions.

## 8. Wartime Economic Stages

### W0 — Tension

- insurance and route risk increase;
- strategic stockpiling;
- capital and skilled people begin moving.

### W1 — Limited conflict

- selected sanctions;
- military procurement and delay of civilian maintenance;
- 1.2–2 × price increases in affected goods.

### W2 — Regional war

- route closures and requisition;
- rationing and wage delay;
- 2–5 × selected prices;
- black markets and substitutes.

### W3 — Theater war

- shipyard/medical overload;
- population movement;
- currency/claim distrust;
- 5–20 × or unavailable strategic goods;
- forced labor/service and emergency administration.

### W4 — Civilizational service collapse

- money loses meaning where physical and institutional capacity fails;
- allocation, identity, route and command become the economy;
- Seed/Aurel gains legitimacy by restoring executable service.

### W5 — Transition/reconstruction

- competing currencies and claims;
- compensation and debt disputes;
- infrastructure and skilled-worker shortages;
- wealthy regions recover faster;
- central equalization versus local control conflict.

## 9. Sanction Scenarios

### Helix parts/certification embargo

Immediate:
- safe repair and warranty stop.

Adaptation:
- open/local parts, cannibalization, worker defection.

Cost:
- failures, slower production, insurance denial and liability.

### Imperial identity/payment freeze

Immediate:
- salary, movement, legal standing and procurement stop.

Adaptation:
- Neutral settlement, local credits, provisional identity.

Cost:
- fragmented recognition and fraud risk.

### Neutral insurance/berth refusal

Immediate:
- ships cannot dock, carry passengers or access certain markets legally.

Adaptation:
- local ports, state guarantees or illegal routes.

Cost:
- seizure, loss, higher casualty and evidence distrust.

### Independence route denial/destruction

Immediate:
- adversary movement blocked.

Cost:
- civilian food/medicine and future local economy;
- political legitimacy and attribution conflict.

### Aurel classification downgrade

Immediate:
- service, route and investment become conditional/deferred.

Adaptation:
- local systems and appeals.

Cost:
- entire regions trapped in low-confidence status.

## 10. Criminal and Gray Economy

Functions:
- move people and goods across denied routes;
- convert currencies and claims;
- forge or borrow identity;
- salvage disputed assets;
- provide medicine and parts outside certification;
- exploit debt, scarcity and fear.

Not one pirate underworld.

Actors:
- legitimate businesses bending rules;
- local officials;
- smugglers and route families;
- salvage houses;
- forged-parts brokers;
- political cells;
- medical/identity intermediaries;
- predatory traffickers.

Story rule:
- gray systems may save people and exploit them simultaneously;
- law enforcement can reduce predation and block survival routes;
- criminal profits follow real bottlenecks.

## 11. Economic Intelligence

Indicators of coming conflict:
- insurance spread;
- route queue and priority changes;
- unusual medicine/food/parts purchases;
- skilled-worker migration;
- military family allotment changes;
- delayed wages;
- currency conversion and local-credit discount;
- warehouse and thermal storage use;
- shipyard bookings;
- identity/claims filings.

P-001’s strategic skill must include these signals, not only fleet sightings.

## 12. Economic Counter-Collection

Factions can deny or acquire targets through:
- buying debt and claims;
- hiring crews/maintainers;
- controlling insurance;
- offering family support;
- reserving route/berth/yard time;
- purchasing substitutes;
- flooding markets;
- freezing payment/identity;
- funding a competing institution.

This supports counter-collection without constant theft or combat.

## 14. Red-Flag Consistency Checks

- Can the buyer actually pay in accepted value?
- Does a route and berth exist?
- Is the good physically available?
- Who insures or bears loss?
- Who operates and maintains it?
- What currency/claim is accepted?
- What is the household or institutional opportunity cost?
- How has war/embargo changed the price?
- Does the transaction create debt, data, loyalty or political obligations?
- Is a non-market allocation more important than price?
