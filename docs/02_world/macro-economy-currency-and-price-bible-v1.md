## 1. Economic Design Principle

The setting uses several currencies and entitlement systems, but the author needs one comparable accounting unit.

Money cannot substitute for:

- route capacity;
- physical food, medicine, parts or fuel;
- authenticated identity and legal standing;
- trained crews and maintainers;
- insurance and liability acceptance;
- political permission;
- time.

A wealthy actor may still be unable to buy a treatment, route slot or repair if the physical and institutional capacity is missing.

## 2. Common Author-Side Unit — Basic Service Credit

Working abbreviation:
- `BSC` — Basic Service Credit / 기초서비스단위.

Definition:

> 1 BSC equals the stable L2-node average cost of one adult’s basic food, potable water, breathable-air recycling share, dormitory-scale habitat energy and sanitation for one day.

It is an author-side comparison basket, not necessarily a coin used in dialogue.

Stable-node baseline includes:
- approximately 2,300–2,600 kcal standard food;
- water and air recycling consumables;
- minimum shared habitation energy and thermal control;
- basic sanitation and waste processing;
- no private room, transport, advanced medicine, entertainment or debt payment.

Baseline household minimum:
- one adult: 30 BSC/month;
- child: 18–24 BSC/month;
- medically dependent adult: 45–120+ BSC/month depending treatment;
- emergency refugee habitat: 40–70 BSC/person/month because of inefficiency and temporary systems.

Price variation:

- productive Core/agricultural node: 0.7–1.1 × BSC basket;
- normal L2/L3 corridor: 1.0–1.5 ×;
- frontier/damaged route: 1.5–3.5 ×;
- siege/embargo: 3–12 × for selected goods;
- total route closure: no meaningful price if supply does not exist.

## 3. Reader-Facing Currency Layers

### 3.1 Imperial Registered Credit — IRC

Use:
- state salary, tax, pension, military pay, public procurement and registered consumer trade.

Strength:
- stable in Core and recognized public institutions.

Weakness:
- requires recognized identity and payment access;
- can be frozen by office/status disputes;
- weaker in informal/frontier markets.

Working conversion at R0:
- 1 IRC ≈ 0.10 BSC.
- reader-facing prices can therefore use tens/hundreds of credits rather than decimals.

### 3.2 Route Settlement Mark — RSM

Use:
- interregional shipping, node fees, cargo, insurance and Neutral settlement.

Nature:
- institutional settlement claim backed by route, insurer and member obligations rather than one state.

Working conversion:
- 1 RSM ≈ 10 BSC under stable conditions.

Strength:
- cross-border acceptance.

Weakness:
- inaccessible to many ordinary people;
- collateral/guarantee and route confidence affect value.

### 3.3 Corporate Contract Credit — CCC

Use:
- Helix wages, treatment, housing, tools and internal purchases.

Nature:
- often convertible but contract-specific deductions and restrictions apply.

Working conversion:
- nominally 1 CCC ≈ 0.10 BSC;
- real external value can fall to 0.05–0.09 BSC after fees or restrictions.

### 3.4 Local Service and Mutual Credits

Use:
- workshop labor, community care, ration pools, local transport and informal settlement.

Value:
- depends on current issuer and community capacity.

Risk:
- cannot always pay external medicine, route fees or legal debt.

### 3.5 Entitlements and Non-Cash Rights

Examples:
- ration allocation;
- housing berth;
- medical eligibility;
- route slot;
- education/placement;
- family guarantee;
- maintenance and rescue priority.

These can be more valuable than cash and can be coercive when tied to employment or service.

## 4. Academy Budget Index Conversion

Existing Academy annual operating budget index:
- `100`.

Working conversion:
- 1 index point = 180,000–240,000 BSC;
- Academy annual budget = 18–24 million BSC.

Recommended design baseline:
- 1 index = 200,000 BSC;
- annual budget = 20 million BSC.

Use:
- large institutional comparisons only;
- do not show the index to readers.

## 5. Wage and Income Basket

Monthly gross income in stable regional nodes:

| Role | BSC/month | IRC equivalent | Notes |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| irregular labor / unstable service | 35–60 | 350–600 | may include housing/food rather than cash |
| basic registered service worker | 60–90 | 600–900 | narrow savings margin |
| skilled industrial worker | 95–160 | 950–1,600 | certification and hazard affect rate |
| experienced maintainer/technician | 130–230 | 1,300–2,300 | high demand, tools/insurance may be separate |
| nurse/medical technician | 120–220 | 1,200–2,200 | region and specialty vary |
| licensed physician/engineer/operator | 220–500 | 2,200–5,000 | liability and scarcity included |
| junior state/military officer | 140–260 | 1,400–2,600 | housing/rations often provided |
| senior officer/administrator | 300–750 | 3,000–7,500 | office expense not personal income |
| ship crew basic share | 80–180 | 800–1,800 | plus voyage/hazard/salvage shares |
| licensed ship master/chief engineer | 250–650 | 2,500–6,500 | debt and claim exposure can exceed salary |

Wage rules:
- employer-provided food/housing/medicine may represent 30–70% of total compensation;
- contract workers can earn high nominal wages while accumulating treatment, tool or housing debt;
- remote/frontier hazard pay is meaningless if supplies and conversion are unavailable;
- military pay delay creates crew loyalty and family survival problems before mutiny.

## 6. Academy Student Credits

Existing student accounting:
- 100 nominal academy credits/month.

Working conversion:
- 100 academy credits ≈ 55–70 BSC gross productive value;
- recommended baseline: 62 BSC.

Typical monthly allocation:

| Charge or allocation | BSC | Equivalent academy credits |
|---|---:|---:|
| food/habitation/equipment | 16–23 | 25–35 |
| family/protector obligation | 9–19 | 15–30 |
| medicine/insurance | 6–16 | 10–25 |
| sentence/debt reserve | 6–16 | 10–25 |
| discretionary | 3–12 | 5–20 |

Result:
- students can be productive without receiving enough spendable income to leave;
- one repair, injury or family crisis can erase months of discretionary credit;
- large mission bonuses are politically powerful but rarely buy clean freedom.

Starting student/family debt:
- common: 1,100–3,700 BSC;
- high medical/family debt: 5,000–12,500 BSC;
- these correspond to several years of net discretionary income, not gross wage.

## 7. Household Examples

### 7.1 Stable worker household

Composition:
- two adults, one child.

Income:
- 190–250 BSC/month combined.

Costs:
- baseline services: 78–90;
- housing above minimum: 25–45;
- transport/communication: 10–20;
- medicine/education: 12–30;
- taxes/insurance/fees: 20–40;
- discretionary/savings: 20–60.

Shock:
- one advanced treatment or job loss can consume years of savings.

### 7.2 Frontier community household

Income:
- 100–220 BSC equivalent, much in local service credits.

Costs:
- imported medicine and parts can be 2–5 × stable price;
- food may be cheap locally but route/identity services expensive;
- mutual obligations substitute for formal insurance.

Risk:
- household looks solvent locally but cannot pay external route or treatment claims.

### 7.3 Helix contract household

Income:
- 180–320 BSC equivalent.

Benefits:
- treatment, housing, tools and education.

Deductions:
- 60–170 BSC/month in contract services and debt.

Exit cost:
- loss of care, housing, certification or data access can exceed saved cash.

### 7.4 Ship crew household

Income:
- irregular voyage pay and family allotment.

Costs:
- delayed pay, route conversion, insurance and casualty support.

A three-month mission failure can threaten family food/housing even if the ship survives.

## 9. Medical Cost Basket

### Basic and emergency

- basic medication/month: 1–8 BSC;
- chronic common medication/month: 5–25;
- emergency trauma stabilization: 25–120;
- short inpatient stay: 40–300;
- decompression/burn/crush recovery: 200–2,500.

### Advanced

- prosthetic maintenance/month: 8–80;
- neural stabilizer/month: 20–250;
- advanced prosthetic replacement: 2,000–20,000;
- complex neural treatment: 4,000–40,000;
- long protected identity/treatment program: 8,000–100,000+.

Why care becomes leverage:
- trained staff and proprietary consumables matter more than purchase price;
- identity/contract status controls eligibility;
- interrupting a treatment may destroy previous investment and threaten life.

Sortie-level costs:
- routine training sortie: 50–400 BSC;

- missile/advanced munition: 100–20,000 each depending class;
- serious frame damage: weeks/months of skilled labor plus parts, often 10,000–300,000+ BSC.

A pilot cannot personally “buy” repeated military sorties from ordinary salary.

## 13. Route, Berth and Cargo Prices

### Passenger and ship passage

- individual neighboring-node basic passage: 10–45 BSC;
- protected medical passage: 50–500+;
- irregular/smuggling passage: can be cheaper cash but higher obligation/risk;
- small ship L3 transition/route fee: 2,000–15,000 BSC;
- regional/trunk priority fee: 10,000–100,000+;
- military/state priority often paid through allocation, not market price.

### Berthing

- cutter/day: 100–600 BSC;
- utility escort/day: 400–2,000;
- frigate/day: 1,000–5,000;
- yard berth and heavy service: 5,000–100,000+ per day depending equipment.

### Cargo

Rates depend on:
- mass and volume;
- life support;
- hazard;
- insurance;
- route queue;
- speed/priority;
- legal identity and destination acceptance.

High-value low-mass cargo:
- medicine, authentication hardware, models, electronics and seeds/cultures.

Low-value strategic bulk:
- water, propellant, structural materials, food biomass and thermal mass.

## 14. Major Interregional Commodity Flows

### Food and biological cultures

Sources:
- Vela-type agricultural rings;
- Garden Chains;
- planetary/agricultural systems.

Destinations:
- Core cities, fleets, medical/industrial habitats and refugee corridors.

Chokepoints:
- fertilizer cultures, water/energy, cold storage, route slots and political allocation.

### Water, volatiles and reaction mass

Sources:
- moons, belts, icy bodies and industrial systems.

Destinations:
- habitats, ships, nodes and thermal systems.

Chokepoints:
- towing, processing, storage and local orbital access.

### Energy and fusion materials

Sources:
- mining/processing systems and large energy infrastructure.

Destinations:
- nodes, yards, fleets, cities and compute/medical systems.

Chokepoints:
- conversion, cooling, storage and specialized reactor parts.

### Advanced materials and electronics

Sources:
- Core and Helix production clusters;
- Brann/Neris and specialist industrial nodes.

Destinations:
- ships, frames, nodes, medicine and AI systems.

Chokepoints:
- rare processes, clean manufacturing, certified design and skilled workers.

### Medicine and biological/technical treatment inputs

Sources:
- Orsen/Helix/public medical networks.

Destinations:
- all populated regions.

Chokepoints:
- identity eligibility, cold chain, sterile production, model/diagnostic access and trained clinicians.

### Data, authentication and compute

Sources:
- Core registries, nodes, Helix, Neutral, regional mirrors and Aurel.

Destinations:
- employers, hospitals, routes, courts, fleets and schools.

Chokepoints:
- trusted keys, bandwidth, correction, privacy and current institutional recognition.

### Skilled labor

Flows:
- engineers, medical staff, node operators, pilots, navigators, teachers and archivists.

Chokepoints:
- training years, family movement, professional recognition, housing and political loyalty.

Labor migration can be more strategically important than hardware capture.

## 15. Trade Chokepoint Classes

### Physical bottleneck

- one yard, port, reactor, storage or route.

### Skilled-worker bottleneck

- equipment exists but certified/experienced people do not.

### Authentication bottleneck

- cargo/person cannot be accepted, paid or insured.

### Capacity bottleneck

- hospital, habitat, berth, route or convoy is full.

### Liability bottleneck

- no institution accepts risk despite technical possibility.

### Political bottleneck

- local population or authority refuses access.

### Information bottleneck

- decisions occur before full evidence arrives.

Major arcs should combine at least two types.

## 16. Tax, Insurance and Debt

### Taxes and public obligations

Stable regions commonly collect:
- 8–20% income/payroll equivalent;
- consumption and berth/route fees;
- employer/service contributions;
- emergency defense or reconstruction levies.

Frontier/local systems often use:
- labor shifts;
- food/repair contributions;
- vessel or route obligations;
- mutual care rather than formal cash tax.

### Insurance

Covers:
- ship/hull and cargo;
- crew injury/death;
- passenger liability;
- medical treatment;
- route interruption;
- industrial failure.

Insurance creates power because it can:
- deny departure;
- demand certified parts;
- influence crew/passenger choices;
- preserve claims across borders.

### Debt

Debt can attach to:
- person wage or contract;
- family/community guarantee;
- ship or equipment lien;
- treatment and housing;
- education/certification;
- public infrastructure and tax revenue.

Legal person ownership is forbidden, but bundles of service, identity, wage and movement claims can approximate coercive control.

## 17. Inflation and Shortage Rules

### Demand shock

- refugees, fleet concentration or failed harvest increases food, housing, medicine and berth costs.

### Supply shock

- route closure, embargo, factory failure or workforce loss.

### Monetary/credit failure

- state/corporate/local claim loses recognition;
- prices may look stable while goods disappear.

### Service inflation

- cash price stays fixed but eligibility, queue time and conditions worsen.

### War inflation bands

- mild disruption: 1.2–1.8 ×;
- regional war: 2–5 × selected goods;
- siege/route closure: 5–20 × or unavailable;
- high-value authentication/medicine can exceed 50 × in black markets.

Do not inflate every good equally.

## 18. Embargo and Sanction Mechanics

Targets:
- parts and updates;
- medicine;
- route and docking;
- insurance;
- identity/payment;
- professional recognition;
- cargo and credit.

Sanctions are effective when:
- the sender controls a genuine bottleneck;
- local substitutes need time or create risk;
- third parties enforce due to liability or politics.

Sanctions fail when:
- alternate routes or standards exist;
- workers defect;
- local legitimacy collapses;
- enforcement costs exceed benefit;
- humanitarian exceptions or smuggling networks expand.

## 19. Fleet and War Economy

A fleet consumes:
- wages and family support;
- food, water, medical and habitat capacity;
- propellant and thermal consumables;
- missiles, countermeasures and parts;
- yard time and skilled labor;
- route capacity;
- insurance/claims and political legitimacy.

Operational rule:
- high-intensity combat can consume months of peacetime budget in days;
- loss of support ships reduces fleet value faster than loss of a few combat hulls;
- unpaid or unsupported crews can withdraw, defect or prioritize families/local regions;
- captured ships increase burden before strength.

## 20. Preservation Regime Economy

Aurel improves:
- payment and pension reliability;
- authenticated contracts;
- fraud reduction;
- route and inventory coordination;
- medical and food allocation;
- military procurement.

Aurel harms or controls through:
- service and identity eligibility;
- priority ranking;
- route investment classification;
- technical certification;
- compulsory contribution;
- frozen claims and review queues.

The regime’s legitimacy is partly economic:
- people support it because pay arrives and hospitals work.

## 21. GA10 Transition Economy

Distribution must transfer:
- budgets;
- tax and contribution authority;
- reserve access;
- contracts and debt;
- payment/identity systems;
- staff and physical assets;
- claims and compensation.

Risks:
- wealthy regions retain reserves;
- poor regions lose equalization;
- local elites capture payroll and assets;
- incompatible currencies/claims disrupt trade;
- central reserve becomes hidden permanent authority.

Bounded central residue may include:
- disaster reserve;
- claims translation;
- temporary equalization;
- catastrophic supply coordination.

It cannot retain universal budget veto or service eligibility.

## 22. Price-Use Rules for Narrative Design

- use exact prices only when a decision depends on the amount;
- otherwise state days/months of wage, ship endurance or household impact;
- compare price to a character’s available cash and non-cash rights;
- show who accepts the currency/claim;
- track inflation and route condition;
- distinguish capital value from operating cost;
- do not let one bounty pay for a fleet without claims, staff and supply.

Reader-friendly conversions:
- meal/day/month of basic service;
- months or years of net income;
- ship-days of operation;
- number of treatment courses;
- route slots or habitat berths.

## 23. Calibration Checks

Academy:
- annual 20 million BSC supports roughly 3,000 residents, training, frames, medicine and infrastructure only because labor, subsidies and contracts share costs.

First ship:
- 7–18 million distressed value and 40,000–100,000 monthly normal operation align with Academy/mission-debt structure.

Community debt:
- 1,100–12,500 BSC is coercive at household/student scale but small relative to ships and institutions.

Frames:
- military frame capital and annual support exceed ordinary lifetime savings.

Fleets:
- large warships and task groups require state/corporate/regional finance, not personal treasure.
